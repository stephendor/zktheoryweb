## Synopsis

In March 1946, men who had spent the war building systems for killing met in New York to discuss systems for living. The first of the Macy Foundation's conferences on feedback mechanisms asked whether the mathematics that aimed an anti-aircraft gun at an evading pilot could also describe a brain, a family, an economy, or a government. Over ten meetings the answer became yes. That answer — that people, like thermostats and guns, are best understood as systems defined by inputs, outputs, and the loops between them — carried a consequence its own most careful inventor, Norbert Wiener, warned against: a feedback loop has target states and error signals, but no variable for need. This chapter follows the loop from Wiener's predictor through Jay Forrester's simulation of urban poverty, the token economies of psychiatric wards, and the conditionality regimes of welfare reform, to Alex Pentland's "social physics," which names Adolphe Quetelet as its ancestor. The tools change at each stage. The question Part I asked — what do people need? — never enters the model.

## 1. From the Bomber to the Boardroom: The Military Birth of Cybernetics

In the autumn of 1940, Norbert Wiener took on a problem for the US National Defense Research Committee that was simple to state and ferocious to solve. Given a noisy radar return from an aircraft flying an evasive course, and a shell that would take many seconds to reach it, predict where the aircraft would be when the shell arrived. The pilot's course was not a formula; it was a person's improvisation under fire.

Wiener's answer, worked out with the engineer Julian Bigelow, was to treat both the noise and the evasion as statistics. He modelled the aircraft's path as a stationary stochastic process — one whose statistical properties stayed stable while its values fluctuated — and derived a filter that produced the best estimate of its future position from the noisy record of its past. His classified 1942 report, bound in yellow and nicknamed the "Yellow Peril," was an engineering document. But the historian Peter Galison saw what lay beneath it. To predict the pilot, Wiener had to treat the pilot as a machine: "Wiener simply treated the pilot-plane assembly as a complex machine — a servomechanism — that once characterized could be simulated in order to predict what it would do. And then it could be blown out of the sky."<sup aria-describedby="sn-1">1</sup>

<Sidenote number={1}>Galison, "The Ontology of the Enemy: Norbert Wiener and the Cybernetic Vision," *Critical Inquiry* (1994), p. 240.</Sidenote>

The move was a technical necessity. Wiener had no access to the cockpit, only to the observed trajectory, so he treated the system as defined entirely by its inputs and outputs — a black box. The necessity became a philosophy. In 1943 Wiener, Bigelow, and the physiologist Arturo Rosenblueth argued in "Behavior, Purpose and Teleology" that purposive behaviour could be defined as negative feedback towards a target state, without reference to intention: a thermostat, a guided torpedo, and a hand reaching for a cup were formally alike. The same year, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts modelled neurons as threshold logic units and the brain as a logical machine. The pilot whose evasions the predictor tracked had, in this picture, a computing machine behind his eyes.

### The Macy Conferences

The Josiah Macy Jr. Foundation's conferences on "Feedback Mechanisms and Circular Causal Systems in Biological and Social Systems," chaired by McCulloch from 1946 to 1953, generalised these wartime ideas across disciplines. Wiener, John von Neumann, Gregory Bateson, Margaret Mead, and others applied feedback, neural formalism, and information theory to brains, families, societies, and ecologies, and each application seemed to confirm the others. If one framework described a thermostat, a nervous system, and a family, it was describing something fundamental about every self-regulating system.

What made the generalisation possible was a decision inside the mathematics. Claude Shannon's 1948 "Mathematical Theory of Communication" defined information as the reduction of uncertainty in a message source and set meaning aside: the semantic aspects of communication, he wrote, were "irrelevant to the engineering problem." N. Katherine Hayles argues that this was the founding erasure. For information to become pure pattern, separable from any medium, "what had to be elided, forgotten, or put in brackets for information to lose its body" was the body itself.<sup aria-describedby="sn-2">2</sup> At the conferences the neurophysicist Donald MacKay pressed the objection: a message's effect depends on the receiver's prior state, so a theory of information cannot abstract away from who receives it. The objection was heard and left unresolved. A theory that needed a model of every receiver was not tractable at scale. Shannon's was universal because it was about nothing in particular.

<Sidenote number={2}>Hayles, *How We Became Posthuman* (1999), p. 2; Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (1948).</Sidenote>

This is the chapter's central claim in its first form. To model a person as a feedback system is to give them a target state, treat deviation as error, and treat error as the occasion for correction. What the person needs, what claims they have, and who chose the target are not suppressed by this framework; they are outside it by construction. Need, in the sense Rowntree meant in York or Orshansky meant when she priced a family's food, is a property of a body in a material circumstance. The cybernetic framework's governing concept is not a body with needs but a system with a target. James Scott's account of high-modernist statecraft — the faith that complex societies can be made legible and then improved by technical intervention — found in cybernetics its rigorous mathematical form: the loop supplied a theory of action, the target a criterion of success, the error signal a diagnosis.<sup aria-describedby="sn-3">3</sup> The same mathematics that had aimed guns now aimed at governance. Different targets.

<Sidenote number={3}>Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998).</Sidenote>

## 2. Wiener's Warning and Its Suppression

Wiener saw the danger before almost anyone. In "A Scientist Rebels," published in the *Atlantic Monthly* in January 1947, he announced that he would no longer supply his work to military research.<sup aria-describedby="sn-4">4</sup> In 1949 he wrote to Walter Reuther of the United Auto Workers to warn that feedback-controlled automation would make large parts of the working class redundant. And in *The Human Use of Human Beings* (1950) he set out the argument systematically.

<Sidenote number={4}>Wiener, "A Scientist Rebels," *Atlantic Monthly* (January 1947).</Sidenote>

A control system applied to a factory measures workers' output against a target, treats deviation as error, and adjusts wages, penalties, and conditions to bring behaviour back to target. The worker occupies the position the enemy pilot occupied in the predictor: a source of signal whose future behaviour is to be estimated and corrected. What the worker needs — pay, rest, dignity, security — is not in the model. Wiener drew the economic conclusion bluntly: automatic machinery was the economic equivalent of slave labour, and labour forced to compete with it would have to accept the conditions of slave labour. "We have modified our environment so radically," he wrote, "that we must now modify ourselves in order to exist in this new environment."<sup aria-describedby="sn-5">5</sup>

<Sidenote number={5}>Wiener, *The Human Use of Human Beings* (1950).</Sidenote>

The technical disagreement underneath was about what information is. Shannon equated it with entropy, a statistical measure indifferent to meaning. Wiener treated it as negentropy — organised pattern resisting disorder, closer to meaning and life than to a probability distribution. On Shannon's account, a welfare claimant's case file and a radar return are the same kind of object, and the claimant's needs are outside the model. On Wiener's, the file is the record of a person trying to hold themselves together against the disorder poverty represents, and need is the model's subject. Warren Weaver, writing alongside Shannon in 1949, tried a rescue: communication had a technical layer, a semantic layer, and a layer of influence on conduct. But only the first produced quantitative tools. The semantic and influential problems stayed in prose, and prose does not write welfare policy.

The version of cybernetics that flourished after 1950 was Shannon's clean, militarily useful one, not Wiener's troubled one. It was taken up at RAND, in the management sciences, and — when President Johnson extended the Pentagon's Planning-Programming-Budgeting System to civilian departments in 1965 — in the government agencies that ran social programmes. *The Human Use of Human Beings* has never been out of print. It has been read, cited, and not acted on.

### Two dissenters

Wiener stands in this history beside Mollie Orshansky. Each built a framework that made possible the system they later criticised. Wiener's wartime mathematics supplied the engine of the control systems he came to call domination; Orshansky's thresholds supplied the measure whose annual movement was handed to a price index in 1969, a decision she later said "tends to freeze the poverty line." Each worked inside an institution — MIT, the Social Security Administration — with its own uses for the work. Each argued that a person cannot be reduced to a position in a formal system: Wiener through negentropy, Orshansky through the food budget. One argument was about physics, the other about food. The structure of the dissent was the same, and so was the outcome. The framework survived its author.

## 3. The System Dynamics of Poverty: Forrester and *Urban Dynamics*

In 1969 Jay Forrester, a professor at MIT's Sloan School of Management and the inventor of system dynamics, published *Urban Dynamics*. He had no background in poverty, housing, or the sociology of American cities. He brought a simulation language, DYNAMO, a set of equations for the stocks and flows of a generic city, and the conviction that any complex system could be modelled by specifying its feedback loops.

The model had three classes of residents — "managerial-professional," "labor," and "underemployed," the last lumping together low-wage workers, the unemployed, and welfare recipients — alongside stocks of housing and of business. Its central loop ran as follows. Low-income housing attracted underemployed migrants; more underemployed residents crowded the labour market and slowed upward mobility; slower mobility raised demand for welfare; more welfare attracted more migrants. The loop amplified itself. The simulations concluded that building low-income housing and raising welfare payments made cities poorer, and that the optimal policy was to demolish low-income housing and build for managers and professionals, attracting the businesses and tax revenue that would eventually benefit everyone. Forrester took the conclusions to Congress, testifying before a House subcommittee on urban growth.

### What the model did not contain

*Urban Dynamics* had no variable for racial discrimination. By 1969 that was not a contested omission. Charles Abrams had documented discrimination in housing; the Kerner Commission had concluded a year earlier that "white institutions created" the ghetto, "white institutions maintain it, and white society condones it"; federal researchers had documented redlining since the 1930s. None of it was in the model. Nor was employer wage suppression, unequal schools, or the cost of getting from low-income neighbourhoods to jobs — the mechanisms that sociologists and urban economists then identified as the main causes of urban poverty. If the structural causes are not in the model, the model cannot find them, and what remains — poor people moving towards housing and welfare — becomes by default the driver of the system.

The conservative conclusions were the conservative assumptions made visible. Migration was specified as large and fast in response to welfare; upward mobility as slow and unresponsive; a healthy city as one with more managers relative to the underemployed. Each was a contested claim about how poverty works. The simulation made them look like findings. This is the conversion Theodore Porter identifies as the political work of quantification: argument moved into an arena where it no longer looks like argument.<sup aria-describedby="sn-6">6</sup> Technical criticism followed. In 1973 Douglass B. Lee Jr.'s "Requiem for Large-Scale Models" listed seven sins of such simulations — hypercomprehensive, gross, hungry, wrongheaded, complicated, mechanical, and expensive — and *Urban Dynamics* displayed all seven.<sup aria-describedby="sn-7">7</sup> Lee's critique effectively ended the first wave of urban simulation. It did so by showing technical inadequacy, after the political work was done.

<Sidenote number={6}>Porter, *Trust in Numbers* (1995).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={7}>Lee, "Requiem for Large-Scale Models," *Journal of the American Institute of Planners* (1973).</Sidenote>

### The displaced question

Rowntree in York and Orshansky at the Social Security Administration both began with households: what does this family need, and does it have enough? Forrester's model inverts the relation. The "underemployed" are not people whose requirements the model characterises; they are a stock in a system of equations, rising and falling with migration and mobility rates. The city's health is defined by the ratio of managers to the underemployed, not by whether the underemployed are housed and fed. "What do these people need?" has been replaced by "what correction will move the system toward its target?" — and the answer to the second, demolish the low-income housing and restrict welfare, is the opposite of anything the first could produce, if it were allowed to be asked. The model has no syntax for it. At this scale of ambition, in Scott's terms, the map does not describe the territory. It replaces it.

## 4. The Behavioural Loop: Skinner, Token Economies, and Welfare Reform

In 1961, at Anna State Hospital in southern Illinois, the psychologists Teodoro Ayllon and Nathan Azrin replaced the conventional regime of a psychiatric ward with a system of tokens. Long-term patients earned tokens for specified behaviours — dressing, attending sessions, cleaning, sitting at the right table — and exchanged them for privileges such as a private sleeping space, recreation, or extra food. Their 1968 book *The Token Economy* became the field's manual, and token economies spread through institutions in the United States and Britain.<sup aria-describedby="sn-8">8</sup> The programme's measures of success were its measures of compliance.

<Sidenote number={8}>Ayllon and Azrin, *The Token Economy: A Motivational System for Therapy and Rehabilitation* (1968).</Sidenote>

B. F. Skinner was not a cybernetician, and his framework used no feedback mathematics. But it performed the same displacement. Behaviour, for Skinner, was explained and controlled by reinforcement history; needs, intentions, and feelings were unobservable and so unscientific. The organism was a transformation from stimulus histories to responses, with nothing in between the science had to attend to — Galison's black box, arrived at from the laboratory. *Walden Two* (1948), published the same year as Wiener's *Cybernetics*, imagined a whole community engineered by reinforcement schedules, its citizens experiencing their conditioning as freedom. The question "what do these people require?" had become "what schedule will produce the target behaviour?"

### Compliance without capability

In 1982 Alan Kazdin's review of the token economy literature reached the finding the programmes' evaluations had not been designed to detect: gains made in institutions often failed to carry over outside them, and maintenance and transfer remained the field's unsolved problems.<sup aria-describedby="sn-9">9</sup> Patients who performed the rewarded behaviours while tokens were at stake frequently reverted when the contingency was removed. The programmes had produced compliance, not capability. That was not incidental. If a person is modelled as a function from stimulus history to behaviour, the intervention will adjust the stimulus history. It cannot reach what was driving the behaviour from the inside — the circumstances that made functioning impossible, the isolation, the distress expressed as the non-compliance the schedule was built to suppress.

<Sidenote number={9}>Kazdin, "The Token Economy: A Decade Later," *Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis* (1982).</Sidenote>

### The loop applied to welfare

The Work Incentive Program, created by the US Social Security Amendments of 1967, required adult recipients of Aid to Families with Dependent Children to register for work or training as a condition of benefit, with benefit reductions for failure. It was tightened in 1971 and successively reformed until the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 added time limits and work requirements across the caseload. Each reform intensified the conditions. None addressed the absence of jobs, the cost of childcare, or the geography of labour markets, because the framework had no variable for structure. It had compliance targets and sanctions.

Britain followed. The New Deal programmes from 1998 made participation in employment support mandatory for jobseekers; the Welfare Reform Act 2012 and Universal Credit consolidated the regime. A claimant must accept a claimant commitment specifying the job-search activity they will undertake — applications, hours, appointments with a work coach — and failure can bring a sanction that removes part or all of the standard allowance for up to six months.<sup aria-describedby="sn-10">10</sup> The claimant commitment is a behavioural contract in the syntax of the token economy: the online journal is the observation record, the work-coach appointment the compliance check, the sanction the token withdrawn. The target — "moving into work" — is set by the department, not the claimant, as the target behaviours at Anna State were set by Ayllon and Azrin.

<Sidenote number={10}>Institute for Fiscal Studies, commentary on the DWP sanctions evaluation (2023), on sanction amounts and maximum duration.</Sidenote>

The evidence follows Kazdin's pattern. The Welfare Conditionality project, a five-year study led from the University of York that followed some 480 claimants from 2013, found that sanctions did little to move people into sustained work and pushed many into hardship and ill health.<sup aria-describedby="sn-11">11</sup> The Department for Work and Pensions' own evaluation reached a similar conclusion. Its draft report on the impact of sanctions found that being sanctioned made people stop claiming Universal Credit but reduced their likelihood of entering paid work. The department declined to publish it, saying it would present a misleading picture, and released it in April 2023 only after Freedom of Information requests and three decisions by the Information Commissioner.<sup aria-describedby="sn-12">12</sup> The regime optimises the compliance signal and leaves the outcome — stable work, adequate income — unmoved, for the reason Kazdin gave in 1982. A claimant who cannot find work because there is none within reach, or because childcare costs more than any available job pays, or because no local employer will accommodate their health, is in the system's model simply a compliance failure. The system has no way to tell wilful non-compliance from the structurally determined kind, because it has no variable for structure. Wiener warned against this in 1950. The loop is closed. The person is still second.

<Sidenote number={11}>Welfare Conditionality: Sanctions, Support and Behaviour Change, ESRC project led by the University of York, final findings report (2018).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={12}>Department for Work and Pensions, *The Impact of Benefit Sanctions on Employment Outcomes* (draft report, released 6 April 2023), with the department's context note.</Sidenote>

## 5. Social Physics Redux: Pentland and the Quantified Community

In 2004, Nathan Eagle and Alex Pentland gave Nokia smartphones to around a hundred students and staff at MIT and logged their calls, Bluetooth proximity, and cell-tower locations for nine months. "Reality Mining," published in 2006, claimed that patterns of interaction extracted from phone data could reveal relationships, routines, and social roles within an organisation.<sup aria-describedby="sn-13">13</sup> Pentland's laboratory went on to fit workers with sensor badges recording the tone and timing of face-to-face conversations. In a Bank of America call centre, by Pentland's own account, rescheduling coffee breaks so that colleagues took them together changed their interaction patterns and raised productivity.

<Sidenote number={13}>Eagle and Pentland, "Reality Mining: Sensing Complex Social Systems," *Personal and Ubiquitous Computing* (2006).</Sidenote>

*Social Physics: How Good Ideas Spread* (2014) synthesised the work, and its title was deliberate. Pentland presented his project as the completion of the one Adolphe Quetelet had begun under the same name in 1835: the application of the tools of physical science to human collectivities, frustrated for nearly two centuries by a shortage of data that sensors and smartphones had now ended.<sup aria-describedby="sn-14">14</sup> This is the chapter's closing case because Pentland supplies the genealogy himself. He does not see in Quetelet what a long line of critics has — that defining the average as optimal and deviation as error is a political act in statistical dress. He sees an unfinished project.

<Sidenote number={14}>Pentland, *Social Physics: How Good Ideas Spread — The Lessons from a New Science* (2014).</Sidenote>

### One structure, three tools

Across Quetelet's average man, Wiener's feedback loop, and Pentland's idea-flow networks, the structure is the same: a model of human collectivities measures the distance between observed and optimal behaviour, and prescribes a correction. What changes is the tool. Quetelet used the normal curve and error theory; Wiener, feedback control; Pentland, network graphs and metrics of "engagement" and "exploration," with the nudge — the rescheduled break, the prompted introduction — as the correction. What does not change is that the subject's material situation is outside the model. Quetelet's average man has no wages or rent. Wiener's loop has no variable for need. Pentland's idea-flow model has no variable for whether better idea flow can change a poor community's circumstances.

That matters most where Pentland applies the framework to poverty. If poor communities have too little "exploration" in their networks, improving idea flow should help them adopt more productive behaviours. The assumption is that the barrier is informational — a shortage of good ideas — rather than material: a shortage of income, assets, infrastructure, or credit. It is precisely the assumption Rowntree's York survey tested and refuted in 1899, when he showed that poverty was produced by wages below a subsistence minimum rather than by the habits his contemporaries preferred to blame. Social physics reinstalls the behavioural explanation with better sensors. Evgeny Morozov's name for the pattern is "solutionism": recasting social and political problems as engineering problems with a technological fix.<sup aria-describedby="sn-15">15</sup> It is cybernetics in Silicon Valley dress. And the semantic problem Shannon deferred is still deferred: a badge records that two people met, and the model infers that an idea moved, with no access to whether the meeting was learning, pressure, or simply a shared break the algorithm had arranged.

<Sidenote number={15}>Morozov, *To Save Everything, Click Here* (2013).</Sidenote>

The workers wearing the badges had not chosen to become research subjects; data collection was a condition of employment with a company that had commissioned the analysis. They occupied the position of Galison's enemy pilot: nodes characterised entirely by what the sensors recorded, with no interior the model was required to attend to, and no means of contesting what was done with the result.

### The structure has not changed

The chapter began in 1946 with men who had built systems for killing turning to the self-regulating society. It ends in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 2014, in a laboratory completing a Belgian astronomer's dream of social physics with the sensors it always lacked. The vocabulary has changed and the institutions have moved from a postwar foundation to corporate-funded labs. The structure has not. A model specifies a target state; behaviour is measured as deviation from it; a correction — policy intervention, reinforcement schedule, network nudge — is applied. The political assumptions in the choice of target and variables are presented as technical parameters. What the people inside the model need remains outside it, because the model was built to answer a different question: how does the system move toward its target? Wiener saw this in 1950, in a book still in print and still not acted on. The tools grow more sophisticated at each stage. The poor are still second.

### Connection Forward

Chapter 6 follows cybernetics' most consequential institutional vehicle into welfare governance — the RAND Corporation's transplant of operations research from the Pentagon to the social policy arena, where the cost-effectiveness objective function replaced the question of material need with the question of programme efficiency.
