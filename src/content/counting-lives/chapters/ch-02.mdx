## Synopsis

In 1884, nine thousand visitors to a South Kensington exhibition paid threepence each to have their bodies measured, and left a duplicate of their results in Francis Galton's files. This chapter follows what Galton, and then Karl Pearson, did with such records. The normal curve, regression to the mean, and the correlation coefficient were real mathematical achievements, and they are still in daily use. But they were developed inside a research programme whose question was hereditary worth: who was eminent, who was mediocre, who was degenerate, and what should be done about it. Again and again, the mathematics described a pattern and the programme supplied a verdict — turning the middle of a distribution into "mediocrity," a regression line into a case for selective breeding, a group of London schoolchildren into evidence about whether Jewish migrants should be admitted, and the poorest stratum of the city into a hereditary "residuum." None of these steps was compelled by the arithmetic. After 1945 the grammar outlived the programme by uneven routes — Spearman's general factor, Burt's disputed twin correlations, the Pioneer Fund's money, *The Bell Curve*, and now commercial scores that rank embryos. That is the point: quantitative form can make a political hierarchy sound like neutral evidence, and the decisions that turn measurement into hierarchy have to be found and named.

## 1. The Threepenny Laboratory: Science as Ideology at South Kensington

In the summer of 1884, visitors to the International Health Exhibition at South Kensington could, for threepence, learn exactly what kind of body they had. The Anthropometric Laboratory occupied a corridor fitted with instruments that Galton had designed, commissioned, or adapted to measure the British public. You paid, submitted to a battery of tests, and received a card printed with your results: height standing and sitting, arm span, weight, strength of pull and squeeze, breathing power, swiftness of blow, visual acuity, colour sense, judgment of eye, hearing, and the highest audible note. The card was yours to keep. What the pamphlet noted only in passing was that a duplicate was "preserved for statistical purposes."<sup aria-describedby="sn-1">1</sup>

<Sidenote number={1}>Galton, *On the Anthropometric Laboratory at the Late International Health Exhibition* (1884).</Sidenote>

One card went home as a souvenir. The other went into Galton's files.

By the end of the exhibition, "no less than 9,337" visitors had passed through. Galton's pamphlet emphasised "the great simplicity of the instruments and methods" and presented the laboratory as a demonstration of technique — educated entertainment, a fairground stall dressed in the authority of brass instruments and printed data.<sup aria-describedby="sn-2">2</sup> The visitor received a quantified version of their own body. Galton kept the material from which population-level comparisons could later be made.

<Sidenote number={2}>Galton 1884.</Sidenote>

### The instruments of legibility

The seventeen tests were not a random selection of bodily attributes. They were chosen to capture traits Galton believed, or hoped, would correlate with what he called "natural ability" — the quality he had been trying to pin down statistically since *Hereditary Genius* fifteen years earlier. The sensory tests followed a bet he had set out the year before in *Inquiries into Human Faculty*: "the more perceptive the senses are of difference, the larger is the field upon which our judgment and intelligence can act."<sup aria-describedby="sn-3">3</sup> The apparatus made sensory acuity a candidate proxy for intelligence. Whether that relationship held was a separate empirical question the instrument list could not answer.

<Sidenote number={3}>Galton, *Inquiries into Human Faculty and Its Development* (1883), p. 19.</Sidenote>

Visitors moved through the corridor station by station, each test administered in moments. The pamphlet boasted that the whole battery took minutes and that the apparatus was portable and the procedure reproducible. This was measurement designed for throughput: Galton wanted a dataset large enough to construct population norms, which meant reducing each body to a row of standardised numbers. James C. Scott's word for this operation is *legibility* — the reorganisation of complex social life into simplified, standard categories that can be centrally observed.<sup aria-describedby="sn-4">4</sup> Nine thousand particular bodies, each with its own history of nutrition, labour, and illness, entered the corridor and emerged as rows in a ledger. The ledger did not record circumstances. It recorded measurements that could be compared.

<Sidenote number={4}>Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998), pp. 2–3.</Sidenote>

This is the move a visitor could easily miss. Measurement produces a number; comparison places it against a chosen reference population; a hierarchy emerges only when someone treats the comparison as evidence of better or worse. The normal curve does not say what its tails mean. Galton's wider programme did.<sup aria-describedby="sn-5">5</sup>

<Sidenote number={5}>MacKenzie, *Statistics in Britain, 1865–1930* (1981).</Sidenote>

### The duplicate card

Most accounts treat the souvenir card as the object of interest — an early "quantified self." But the card that mattered was the one the visitor never saw again. A visitor who read a grip strength of forty-seven pounds understood it, if at all, as a fact about themselves. To Galton it was one point in a distribution, to be aggregated with thousands of others in the search for patterned variation. Ian Hacking's argument that statistical categories bring new kinds of people into being clarifies what the duplicate accomplished: the cards made a reference distribution possible, and once an institution treats a distribution as a norm, it can identify deviations and attach claims to them.<sup aria-describedby="sn-6">6</sup> The cards alone did not establish hereditary fitness. Without archived, comparable observations, though, Galton could not have made the population-wide comparisons his programme needed.

<Sidenote number={6}>Hacking, "Making Up People" (1986), pp. 231–32.</Sidenote>

### Entertainment as authority

The arrangement solved, in a single installation, a problem that would trouble large-scale data collection for the next century and a half: how to get a large population to submit to measurement. Galton made it entertaining, and charged a fee low enough to draw thousands while signalling that the experience had value. The fee made the visitor a customer. You paid; you were measured; you received your card; you left. The analytical work depended on the duplicate left behind.

Theodore Porter calls quantification a "technology of distance": numbers gain authority because they are impersonal, substituting rule-following for personal judgment.<sup aria-describedby="sn-7">7</sup> The laboratory was that thesis in physical form. A dynamometer records grip strength regardless of who squeezes it, and the number carries no trace of the decision that made grip strength worth recording. By the time the numbers appeared in Galton's publications — arranged in distributions, analysed for correlations, read as evidence of hereditary pattern — they had passed through the impersonal authority of mechanical measurement. To contest the conclusions you had to contest the numbers; to contest the numbers you had to contest the instruments; and the instruments were described in meticulous detail in the pamphlet, for anyone who cared to look.

<Sidenote number={7}>Porter, *Trust in Numbers* (1995), p. ix.</Sidenote>

When the exhibition closed, Galton moved the apparatus to the South Kensington Museum. The move changed the laboratory from a curiosity into an institution with a durable archive. After Galton's death in 1911, the Galton Laboratory for National Eugenics at University College London, directed by Karl Pearson, gave biometric methods a permanent home.<sup aria-describedby="sn-8">8</sup> The laboratory's own findings were thin, and largely inconclusive on whether sensory acuity tracked intelligence. Its contribution was structural: it showed that voluntary participation, mechanical measurement, and aggregation could yield a reference distribution. Donald MacKenzie's central argument is that Galton's and Pearson's statistical innovations were co-produced with the eugenic questions they addressed. The corridor makes that co-production visible: the instruments built the archive; the programme supplied the hereditary reading.

<Sidenote number={8}>MacKenzie 1981.</Sidenote>

## 2. From Quetelet's Average to Galton's Hierarchy

Before Galton, the normal curve belonged to Adolphe Quetelet. In 1835 the Belgian astronomer published *Sur l'homme et le développement de ses facultés*, proposing a social physics: measure a human trait across a large enough population — height, chest circumference, the yearly propensity to commit crimes — and the distribution would follow the same bell curve astronomers used to model observational error. Its mean was *l'homme moyen*, the average man, around whom individual deviations scattered with lawful regularity.<sup aria-describedby="sn-9">9</sup>

<Sidenote number={9}>Quetelet, *Sur l'homme et le développement de ses facultés, ou essai de physique sociale* (1835).</Sidenote>

Quetelet's concept was descriptive. He wanted to explain why murder rates, marriage rates, and even the number of letters reaching the Paris dead-letter office held steady from year to year. Deviations from the mean were what astronomers took them to be: fluctuation around a signal. He sometimes drifted towards idealisation — Alain Desrosières describes his average man as "a kind of prudent centrist, who avoided every conceivable form of excess" — but the drift was secondary.<sup aria-describedby="sn-10">10</sup> Quetelet did not build a politics on the tails of his distribution.

<Sidenote number={10}>Desrosières, *The Politics of Large Numbers* (1998).</Sidenote>

### The transformation

Galton inherited the curve and performed a transformation that was mathematically slight and politically enormous. He made the average a failure.

His word was "mediocrity" — from *mediocritas*, the middle state, already shadowed in English by inadequacy. In *Hereditary Genius* (1869), the normal distribution is a grading system. The right tail holds the eminent; the left, the degenerate; the middle is not the commonest condition of a healthy population but the place ordinary people settle when nothing lifts them. Galton was explicit about the symmetry: "eminently gifted men are raised as much above mediocrity as idiots are depressed below it."<sup aria-describedby="sn-11">11</sup> The mathematical object was Quetelet's. The interpretation was new. Quetelet's average man was a centre of gravity; Galton's mediocre man measured what the population wasted by failing to breed selectively. A normal curve does not tell us whether its tails are pathological. The curve is a shape; pathology is a decision.

<Sidenote number={11}>Galton, *Hereditary Genius* (1869), p. 36.</Sidenote>

### Counting privilege

*Hereditary Genius* drew its "eminent" men from biographical dictionaries, legal records, lists of Royal Society fellows, senior clergy, commanders, statesmen, and literary figures, then counted how often they had eminent fathers, sons, brothers, and nephews. Eminence clustered in families more than chance predicted; Galton inferred that "natural ability" was inherited. Stephen Jay Gould identified the two foundational errors: *reification*, treating eminence — a designation conferred by institutions — as a measurable property of individuals; and *ranking*, arranging human beings on a single scale from degenerate to eminent and letting the curve make the scale look natural.<sup aria-describedby="sn-12">12</sup>

<Sidenote number={12}>Gould, *The Mismeasure of Man* (1981).</Sidenote>

The sample was built by the conclusion it was meant to support. A man became a judge because his father was a barrister, because his grandfather had the money to educate his sons, because the family owned property whose income paid for the credentials Galton counted as evidence of natural ability. To call this hereditary genius is to mistake the social reproduction of a class for the biological transmission of a trait. MacKenzie is careful to concede that Galton's arithmetic was competent. His point is that the questions, categories, and interpretation came from Galton's position — independently wealthy, a grandson of Erasmus Darwin, educated at Trinity College, Cambridge — studying the heritability of elite status from inside the elite, through the elite's own institutions.<sup aria-describedby="sn-13">13</sup>

<Sidenote number={13}>MacKenzie 1981.</Sidenote>

### The invisible women

The dataset of *Hereditary Genius* is entirely male, because its categories of eminence — judges, statesmen, commanders, men of science, divines — were closed to Victorian women. Women appear in the pedigree tables only as connective tissue: the mothers, wives, and daughters through whom eminent lineages pass. Galton's 1897 paper on "The Average Contribution of each several Ancestor to the total Heritage of the Offspring" treated maternal and paternal inheritance symmetrically — each parent a quarter, each grandparent a sixteenth.<sup aria-describedby="sn-14">14</sup> The algebra was gender-neutral. The data was not. Women were necessary to the equations as transmitters of hereditary value and absent from the evidence as bearers of their own ability. The ranked distribution of British genius was a ranked distribution of British men, generalised to humanity by silence rather than argument.

<Sidenote number={14}>Galton, "The Average Contribution of each several Ancestor to the total Heritage of the Offspring," *Proceedings of the Royal Society* (1897).</Sidenote>

### Distance from the mean

The modern z-score makes the distinction concrete, though Galton never used the term. It is a standardised distance from a reference mean, not a rank in itself. A test score of 85, in a reference group with mean 70 and standard deviation 10, has a z-score of $(85 - 70)/10 = 1.5$. If the reference distribution is roughly normal, that corresponds to about the ninety-third percentile. The calculation becomes a ranking only when an institution chooses the reference population and treats relative position as evidence of ability, need, or worth. Galton's political move was to make distance from the mean signify mediocrity, eminence, or degeneracy. The arithmetic did not dictate that meaning.

Mathematical form mattered for another reason. An opinion about hereditary ability can be met with other opinions. A published dataset with expected and observed rates shifts the dispute onto technical ground, where only readers able to interrogate the method can challenge it. And a statistical finding is portable: it can be cited in a report and applied in another country. Eugenic institutions and advocates later carried Galton's vocabulary into pedigree work at the Eugenics Record Office at Cold Spring Harbor, campaigns for immigration restriction, and American sterilisation policy — though each of those projects required its own funding, legal authority, and political choices, not a single automatic chain of causation from 1869.<sup aria-describedby="sn-15">15</sup> The form travels. Institutions decide what it will authorise.

<Sidenote number={15}>Kevles, *In the Name of Eugenics* (1995); Porter 1995.</Sidenote>

## 3. Regression as Destiny: The Mathematical Case for Selective Breeding

Galton's most consequential mathematical discovery began with sweet peas. In the late 1870s he sent packets of seeds of different sizes to friends and correspondents, asked them to grow the plants, and measured the seeds they returned. Large seeds produced offspring that were, on average, larger than the population mean — but not as large as their parents. Small seeds produced small offspring, less extremely so. Extreme values in one generation produced less extreme values in the next. Galton called this "regression toward mediocrity." He found the same pattern in human height: unusually tall parents had children who were on average shorter than themselves, unusually short parents children who were taller. Plot parent against offspring and the fitted line slopes towards the mean with a slope less than one. That paired prediction does not mean the population as a whole is converging on a single height.

The finding is genuine and still in use. Regression to the mean is a statistical phenomenon, not a biological one: it appears in any paired measurement whose correlation is less than perfect. A student who scores exceptionally well on one exam will, on average, score less exceptionally on the next. A city with an unusually high crime rate one year will, on average, have a lower rate the next, whether or not any policy has changed. That is what we know now. What Galton chose to know was different.

### The eugenic reading

Galton read regression as a warning. If the children of exceptional parents are less exceptional, the gains of one generation are diluted in the next; without intervention, the population stays centred on mediocrity. If superior individuals bred with one another rather than with the average, the regression line would steepen and the right tail thicken. The programme he proposed — for which he coined the word "eugenics" in *Inquiries into Human Faculty* in 1883 — was the systematic encouragement of reproduction among the "fit" and its discouragement among the "unfit."<sup aria-describedby="sn-16">16</sup> The mathematical justification was regression. The political programme was breeding. Nothing in the slope of a parent–offspring line says that the extremes ought to be preserved or amplified. A tendency can be lamented, celebrated, or simply noted. Galton lamented it, and the lament was not in the data. It was in a reader whose place at the top of the Victorian class hierarchy gave him a personal interest in the conclusion that the top of the distribution was where value lay.

<Sidenote number={16}>Galton 1883.</Sidenote>

### Pearson's formalisation

Karl Pearson gave Galton's graphical work an algebraic form. His 1896 memoir described the task as an expansion of Galton's ideas, "particularly their application to the problem of bi-parental inheritance," and developed general treatments of regression and the product-moment correlation coefficient *r*.<sup aria-describedby="sn-17">17</sup> The coefficient runs from −1 through 0 to +1. A correlation of 0.5 between parent and offspring height means that taller-than-average parents tend to have taller-than-average children while leaving much of the variation unexplained. It cannot say why — whether the relationship is genetic, environmental, social, or all three.

<Sidenote number={17}>Pearson, "Mathematical Contributions to the Theory of Evolution. III. Regression, Heredity, and Panmixia," *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A* (1896), pp. 255, 259, 265.</Sidenote>

Pearson's paper nonetheless called the correlation of parent and offspring organs "the mathematical measure of heredity." It worked with averages and probabilities for large classes, because the causes acting on any one person were too complex to treat exactly — a population-level framing that suited a programme intent on measuring hereditary resemblance. He modelled correlation through normal-correlation surfaces, while cautioning that such a surface was "only an approximation."<sup aria-describedby="sn-18">18</sup> A Pearson coefficient can now be calculated without that assumption, and it remains a one-number summary of linear association, not a proof of mechanism. The historical question is why the first extended formal treatment made hereditary resemblance its central problem, and what kinds of policy claim that made easier to frame.

<Sidenote number={18}>Pearson 1896, pp. 255–56, 259, 298.</Sidenote>

### The Biometric Laboratory and Alice Lee

Pearson's base was the Biometric Laboratory at University College London, established with Galton's support. He founded *Biometrika* in 1901 as its journal. With collaborators including Alice Lee, the laboratory computed correlations and regressions for father–son, father–daughter, mother–son, and mother–daughter pairs, turning family relations into an apparatus for estimating hereditary transmission. In those 1903 tables, mothers' measurements matter because they enter a calculation of descent.<sup aria-describedby="sn-19">19</sup>

<Sidenote number={19}>Pearson and Lee, "On the Laws of Inheritance in Man: I. Inheritance of Physical Characters," *Biometrika* (1903).</Sidenote>

Lee's own 1901 study complicates any simple account of the laboratory's gendered division of labour. The paper was hers — Pearson wrote that its reductions and inferences were "substantially her work." She used correlation and regression to test the claim that skull capacity tracked intellectual power, measuring thirty-five living anatomists, twenty-five University College staff, and thirty women of Bedford College. Her conclusion was unequivocal: "no marked correlation between skull capacity and intellectual power" in either sex.<sup aria-describedby="sn-20">20</sup> The result did not end craniometry or redirect the laboratory. It made a narrower and more important point: the same apparatus could disconfirm a claim that animated it. Which relationships to measure, and what to conclude from them, remained political choices.

<Sidenote number={20}>Lee, "Data for the Problem of Evolution in Man. VI. A First Study of the Correlation of the Human Skull," *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A* (1901), pp. 225, 255–59.</Sidenote>

### The tool and its afterlife

The product-moment coefficient is now one of the most widely used statistical tools in the world. A public-health researcher correlates air pollution with asthma admissions; a labour economist, years of education with lifetime earnings; a welfare analyst, benefit sanctions with employment outcomes. The coefficient is taught as a technique rather than a history, and its early place in a hereditary programme has receded from view. MacKenzie's conclusion is blunt: "Without eugenics, statistical theory would not have developed in the way it did in Britain — and indeed might not have developed at all, at least till much later."<sup aria-describedby="sn-21">21</sup> That history does not make a welfare analyst a eugenicist. It does make one question unavoidable for any correlation put before an institution: what does this one-number summary make visible, what does it conceal, and what decision is about to be made from it?

<Sidenote number={21}>MacKenzie 1981, p. 221.</Sidenote>

## 4. The Colonial Ledger: Ranking at Imperial Scale

The laboratory ranked individuals within a population; *Hereditary Genius* ranked families within a class. Eugenic thought also supplied a language for ranking groups against one another — who was fit to govern, to labour, to settle, or to be excluded. Two episodes show it at work at different scales. They are not one uninterrupted programme, and the people they classified were not interchangeable. What links them is a shared move: treating a political decision about membership, territory, and power as an assessment of a population's capacities.

### "Africa for the Chinese"

In 1873 Galton published a letter in *The Times* under the title "Africa for the Chinese." He proposed that British policy encourage large-scale Chinese settlement on the East African coast, where Chinese settlers would "supplant the inferior Negro race" and make supposedly unproductive territory productive.<sup aria-describedby="sn-22">22</sup> The letter is advocacy, not a statistical study. Its importance is that it makes the hierarchy behind the later measurements unusually explicit. Two peoples become entries in an imperial calculation, assigned unequal capacities for industry, settlement, and reproduction, and the displacement of one by the other is entered as an improvement in the civilisational account. The laboratory and the letter do not mechanically produce each other. Both make human worth appear available for technical assessment, and both turn that assessment into a proposed order of administration.

<Sidenote number={22}>Galton, "Africa for the Chinese," *The Times*, 5 June 1873.</Sidenote>

### When the imperial grammar came home

Pearson and Margaret Moul's 1925 study did not concern a colonial population. It examined Russian and Polish Jewish children in London, and its declared question was metropolitan: whether Britain should permit what the authors called indiscriminate immigration, and on what grounds it might discriminate. The paper framed this as a problem of "national eugenics," asking whether prospective migrants should meet standards of physique and mentality.<sup aria-describedby="sn-23">23</sup>

<Sidenote number={23}>Pearson and Moul, "The Problem of Alien Immigration into Great Britain, Illustrated by an Examination of Russian and Polish Jewish Children," Part I, *Annals of Eugenics* 1(1) (1925), pp. 5–8.</Sidenote>

The authors assembled dossiers on about 600 boys and nearly as many girls at the Jews' Free School in Aldgate, combining school, medical, and home-visitor schedules. They recorded bodies, family circumstances, and teachers' assessments of intelligence on the Biometric Laboratory's scale, which the article presented as a measure of innate mental power rather than acquired knowledge. The children were classified as "alien" even though, by the paper's own count, 71.7 per cent had been born in England and were English nationals in law.<sup aria-describedby="sn-24">24</sup> A socially and historically situated group of schoolchildren was converted into a racial population whose alleged properties bore on the admission of others.

<Sidenote number={24}>Pearson and Moul 1925, Part I, pp. 9–11.</Sidenote>

The study did make comparisons — with London County Council schools in good districts, poor districts, and London as a whole, and with material from Leeds. The troubling point is what the comparisons were made to carry. When the authors found similar growth patterns among Jewish children in London and Leeds, they read the result as evidence against Franz Boas's account of environmental modification and for racial growth curves. The force of environment was present in the material; the interpretive framework decided what counted as an explanation. Part I ends by saying that a more favourable judgment might follow only if the children proved markedly superior in intelligence to native Gentiles.<sup aria-describedby="sn-25">25</sup> The standard had been set before the intelligence data were reported: in a crowded country, admission was to be earned by superiority. Part II supplied the notorious verdict: "Taken on the average, and regarding both sexes, this alien Jewish population is somewhat inferior physically and mentally to the native population."<sup aria-describedby="sn-26">26</sup>

<Sidenote number={25}>Pearson and Moul 1925, Part I, pp. 25–28, 51.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={26}>Pearson and Moul 1925, Part II, *Annals of Eugenics* 1(2), pp. 125–26.</Sidenote>

### A method chosen for its answer

The paper had a method: schedules, medical examinations, anthropometry, school records, comparative tables, correlations between parental literacy and children's intelligence, age and growth. Its defect lay in the policy rule that organised all of it. The children were asked to bear a burden no resident population bears — to show that they, or the migrants they were made to stand for, were superior enough to merit entry. Tables can record patterns. They cannot decide whether a political community should attach rights and belonging to those patterns.

The authors were candid about their self-understanding. The immigration question, they wrote, could be resolved only in the "cold light of statistical inquiry," and the Galton Laboratory had no political, religious, or social prejudice.<sup aria-describedby="sn-27">27</sup> The passage performs objectivity as well as claiming it. In Porter's terms the political decision was not erased but relocated: the choice to make physique, mentality, and presumed hereditary capacity into entry criteria was made before any calculation began, and once the question took statistical form, debate was channelled towards samples, measures, and coefficients. The children at the Jews' Free School entered the record as cases — assessments, examinations, family data, and home visits clipped into dossiers and reduced to distributions that spoke in the name of immigration policy. Their authority came from numbers, categories, the institutional standing of the Galton Laboratory, and the name of Karl Pearson.

<Sidenote number={27}>Pearson and Moul 1925, Part I, p. 8; Porter 1995.</Sidenote>

## 5. The Residuum: When Statistics Meets the Poor Law

Galton's *Essays in Eugenics* (1909) contains a small act of translation. He took Charles Booth's Class A from the survey of East London and placed it in "the lowermost third of our 'v and below'."<sup aria-describedby="sn-28">28</sup> A category of urban insecurity had become a location in a hereditary distribution.

<Sidenote number={28}>Galton, *Essays in Eugenics* (1909), p. 20.</Sidenote>

English Poor Law administration had long separated those held unable to work from those treated as unwilling, so poverty was always a moral question as well as a material one. Booth's *Life and Labour of the People* supplied a precursor, though not a biological theory. He called Class A "the lowest class," its number "beyond enumeration," and wrote that it was "now hereditary to a very considerable extent" — yet the surrounding pages treat the class as shaped by economic conditions, charity, and movement between classes.<sup aria-describedby="sn-29">29</sup> The hereditary phrase cannot bear the eugenic reading on its own. Galton supplied that reading, and eugenic writers recast the *residuum* — a stigmatised stratum of the urban poor — as constitutionally different from the respectable working class: poor because they supposedly lacked the hereditary capacity to sustain themselves, with children who would be poor for the same reason.

<Sidenote number={29}>Booth, *Life and Labour of the People* (1889), pp. 37–38.</Sidenote>

### The statistical move

Under the old Poor Law the distinction between deserving and undeserving was made by people: magistrates, relieving officers, workhouse masters who saw applicants face to face and formed judgments about their character and circumstances. Those judgments were inconsistent, local, and contestable. A relieving officer in one parish might grant outdoor relief to a family that his counterpart in the next would send to the workhouse. To central administration the inconsistency was the system's weakness. To applicants it was also its humanity, because an inconsistent system can be appealed to, argued with, persuaded.

Eugenic writers proposed to stabilise the inconsistency through classification and measurement. If the residuum was a hereditary category, identifiable by position in a distribution of hereditary quality, then the old moral distinction could be presented as a matter of data, and the relieving officer's judgment — with all its inconsistency and all its capacity for mercy — subordinated to a statistician's ranking. This is Porter's depoliticisation at its most consequential. A question that parishes had argued about and administered differently became a statistical fact removed from contestation. If the residuum was hereditary, welfare could not change it. If the left tail was biological, redistribution was futile. The mathematics was made to prescribe the limits of what could be done.

### Royal Commission, Mental Deficiency Act, Wood Committee

Eugenic writers took up the debates around the 1909 Royal Commission on the Poor Laws, which divided into a Majority Report recommending a reformed Poor Law and a Minority Report, drafted principally by Beatrice Webb, calling for its abolition and replacement by social services. Neither report was a eugenic programme. Eugenicists nonetheless supplied a vocabulary — the residuum, the degenerate, the social problem group — and a style of inference that represented a political distinction as a scientific one. The Mental Deficiency Act 1913 created four statutory classes — "idiots," "imbeciles," "feeble-minded persons," and "moral imbeciles" — and, in prescribed circumstances, enabled institutional placement, guardianship, and supervision.<sup aria-describedby="sn-30">30</sup> Its categories did not derive formally from a normal curve, but it drew social classification into the same administrative effort: converting a contested position into a condition for the state to manage.

<Sidenote number={30}>Mental Deficiency Act 1913 (3 & 4 Geo. 5 c. 28), ss. 1–3, 30.</Sidenote>

The interwar descendant was the "social problem group," a category used by eugenicists and, increasingly, by mainstream social-policy researchers to designate families alleged to account for a disproportionate share of pauperism, crime, mental deficiency, and public expenditure. The Wood Committee's 1929 report, issued jointly by the Board of Education and the Board of Control, described a "subnormal or social problem group, representing approximately 10 per cent of the whole population," and drew the hereditary inference at once: "the dull children of the present generation … are the potential parents of many feeble-minded in the next generation."<sup aria-describedby="sn-31">31</sup> The ten per cent was a claim about a constructed social group, not an estimate that one person in ten met the statutory definition of mental deficiency. That is exactly what a percentage does: it turns a contested category into a population object that can be counted, costed, and administered.

<Sidenote number={31}>Board of Education and Board of Control, *Report of the Mental Deficiency Committee* (Wood Report, 1929), p. 93.</Sidenote>

The sequence recurs. A moral distinction is recast as a statistical category; the category is named — residuum, social problem group; the name is attached to a number — ten per cent, the left tail, a calculable burden; the number is published in a report, the report is cited, and policy proceeds as though the category were a fact rather than a construction. Who decided that this was a category, who drew the line, and what evidence showed that the line tracked biology rather than political preference all recede from view.

### The long shadow

After 1945, poverty debates used different categories, and later accounts should not be collapsed into eugenic language. Keith Joseph's 1972 account of a "cycle of deprivation," for example, arose in a different political setting and used a different vocabulary.<sup aria-describedby="sn-32">32</sup> The normal curve's left tail, the residuum, the social problem group, and the underclass are historically distinct, not steps in a single institutional lineage. Each can, however, turn poverty into a property of a group and make state intervention look like population management rather than social provision.

<Sidenote number={32}>Joseph, speech to the Pre-School Playgroups Association, 29 June 1972.</Sidenote>

The claim that the poor are a different kind of people predates statistics. What the eugenic ledger added was a mathematical grammar — the ranked distribution, the hereditary inference, the statistical authority — that moved contestation onto the data, definitions, and distributions that produced the ranking. A relieving officer's judgment could be challenged by a neighbour's testimony. A position in a ranked distribution can be challenged only by contesting the category, its evidence, and its calculation: resources those assigned to its left tail seldom possess.

## 6. The Ledger's Long Life: From *Biometrika* to *The Bell Curve*

The Second World War discredited eugenic policy and racial science. By then the statistical repertoire formed around them had already travelled beyond explicit eugenics, along with some of the institutions and arguments that sustained it. The connections that follow take several forms — technical inheritance, contested authority, private patronage, public restatement, and contemporary analogy — and conflating them would flatten the history.

### Spearman's *g*

In 1904 Charles Spearman published "'General Intelligence,' Objectively Determined and Measured" in the *American Journal of Psychology*. Looking at the positive correlations among children's scores on different tests, he proposed a common function, provisionally called general intelligence, within an early two-factor account of mental performance.<sup aria-describedby="sn-33">33</sup> This was correlation-based work later absorbed into factor-analytic practice — not modern principal-components analysis, and not a calculation of an individual's loading on a settled property. It was nonetheless a move of compression. Several tests yield several scores; their correlations make a common factor seem available as an explanation; a spread of performances becomes a rank carrying a claim about a person. The parallel with Galton is analytical rather than institutional: Spearman's paper was mathematical psychology, not a proposal for eugenic policy. Gould names the danger as reification — treating a statistical abstraction as a unitary, biological property.<sup aria-describedby="sn-34">34</sup> A factor can summarise variation across a particular battery of tests without establishing a single physical cause, and without making the resulting rank an account of a whole person. The choice of tests matters; so does the use to which the rank is put. That is where psychometrics meets governance again.

<Sidenote number={33}>Spearman, "'General Intelligence,' Objectively Determined and Measured," *American Journal of Psychology* (1904), pp. 272–73.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={34}>Gould 1981.</Sidenote>

### Burt and verification

Cyril Burt held an influential place in British educational psychology, within a wider field that shaped the arguments and practices of selective schooling. He did not design the 11-plus or the tripartite system alone; those arrangements were made through policy, administration, teaching, and examinations as well as psychology.<sup aria-describedby="sn-35">35</sup> In a 1966 study Burt reported correlations for identical twins reared apart of .771 on a group test, .883 on individual testing, and .874 for a final assessment, and treated them as evidence of a strong hereditary contribution to intelligence — while conceding that what he had calculated was "a somewhat artificial abstraction."<sup aria-describedby="sn-36">36</sup>

<Sidenote number={35}>Wooldridge, *Measuring the Mind: Education and Psychology in England c.1860–c.1990* (1994).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={36}>Burt, "The Genetic Determination of Differences in Intelligence," *British Journal of Psychology* (1966), pp. 139, 147.</Sidenote>

The later controversy brought the record itself into view. Leon Kamin's criticism of the correlations, L. S. Hearnshaw's adverse biography, and N. J. Mackintosh's re-examination leave Burt's data as a contested historical object rather than a settled foundation.<sup aria-describedby="sn-37">37</sup> The dispute need not be settled here. It shows that a coefficient reported to three decimal places cannot substitute for traceable data, methods, and provenance. Challenging it depends on access to the records that produced the number and the capacity to judge whether its precision means anything. Where tests and categories assign children's educational futures, those conditions are part of the politics of measurement.

<Sidenote number={37}>Kamin, *The Science and Politics of I.Q.* (1974); Hearnshaw, *Cyril Burt, Psychologist* (1979); Mackintosh (ed.), *Cyril Burt: Fraud or Framed?* (1995).</Sidenote>

### The Pioneer Fund

The Pioneer Fund supplies a different kind of continuity: private money. Wickliffe Preston Draper incorporated it in 1937 with a charter rooted in heredity, eugenics, and "race betterment." It later supported researchers including Arthur Jensen, J. Philippe Rushton, and Richard Lynn, whose work renewed arguments about race and intelligence.<sup aria-describedby="sn-38">38</sup> Its importance is institutional rather than genealogical. Funding can keep a research programme available after its claims have lost wider legitimacy; the fund preserved a space in which hereditarian arguments could be financed, published, and returned to public debate. That does not make every later paper, dataset, or policy a descendant of Galton's laboratory.

<Sidenote number={38}>Tucker, *The Funding of Scientific Racism: Wickliffe Draper and the Pioneer Fund* (2002).</Sidenote>

### *The Bell Curve*

Richard Herrnstein and Charles Murray's *The Bell Curve* (1994) returned intelligence, hierarchy, and social policy to a mass audience. It drew on a repertoire of standardised cognitive distributions, heritability claims, correlations, and factor models with historical links to Galton, Pearson, and Spearman — which is not the same as making the book a direct institutional descendant of any of them.<sup aria-describedby="sn-39">39</sup> Claude Fischer and his co-authors challenged its construction of intelligence, its treatment of racial categories, its causal inferences, and its policy conclusions.<sup aria-describedby="sn-40">40</sup> The dispute shows a familiar asymmetry. A distribution, a coefficient, and a statistical appendix can give a political argument the look of technical finality. Contesting it means examining the models, variables, and inferences inside the calculation rather than accepting its polished numerical surface.

<Sidenote number={39}>Herrnstein and Murray, *The Bell Curve: Intelligence and Class Structure in American Life* (1994).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={40}>Fischer, Hout, Jankowski et al., *Inequality by Design: Cracking the Bell Curve Myth* (1996).</Sidenote>

### The ledger continues

Polygenic embryo testing (PGT-P) gives the comparison a contemporary form. A polygenic score aggregates many small genetic associations into a probabilistic estimate of disease risk or some other trait. One provider, Herasight, markets embryo scores for disease-risk screening and presents intelligence among the traits such testing can address.<sup aria-describedby="sn-41">41</sup> Professional bodies are far more cautious. The American Society for Reproductive Medicine's ethics and practice committees describe PGT-P as nascent and unproven, say it should not be offered clinically, and reject its use for selecting non-medical traits; in the United Kingdom, the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority has confirmed that it is not lawful at all.<sup aria-describedby="sn-42">42</sup>

<Sidenote number={41}>Herasight, "What Is PGT-P? Polygenic Embryo Screening Explained" (18 February 2026).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={42}>Klipstein et al., "Use of Preimplantation Genetic Testing for Polygenic Disorders (PGT-P): An Ethics Committee Opinion," *Fertility and Sterility* 125 (2026), pp. 24–30; HFEA, "PGT-P is not lawful in the UK, is not supported by evidence and may reduce the chances of having a baby overall" (2025).</Sidenote>

State eugenics organised coercion through law, institutions, and public administration. Commercial PGT-P works through clinical markets, prospective parents' choices, and a score that ranks possible embryos. The difference matters. The comparison asks what happens when a statistical prediction is invited to stand for a future person's prospects; it does not make every parent, clinician, or geneticist an heir to Galton.

These are unequal relations. Spearman supplies a technical inheritance; Burt shows authority under audit; the Pioneer Fund supplies private infrastructure; *The Bell Curve* restates hierarchy through a later psychometric repertoire; polygenic embryo scoring offers a commercial analogy about ranking and selection. In each case a distribution becomes politically consequential through the work assigned to it, the categories selected for it, and the futures that become easier to administer once a rank has been given.

### Connection Forward

Chapter 3 follows the actuarial revolution — the moment when the welfare state replaced the Poor Law's moral assessment with the contribution record and the eligibility threshold. Where the eugenic tradition ranked populations and drew policy conclusions from the rank, the actuarial tradition classified them and derived entitlements from the classification. Both operations translated political decisions into mathematical form; the political decisions they embedded were different, and so were the exclusions they produced.
