## Synopsis

In 1884, nine thousand visitors to a South Kensington exhibition paid threepence each to have their bodies measured, and left a duplicate of their results in Francis Galton's files. This chapter follows what Galton, and then Karl Pearson, did with such records. The normal curve, regression to the mean, and the correlation coefficient were real mathematical achievements, still in daily use. But they were developed inside a research programme whose question was hereditary worth: who was eminent, who was mediocre, who was degenerate, and what should be done about it. Again and again, the mathematics described a pattern and the programme supplied a verdict — turning the middle of a distribution into "mediocrity," a regression line into a case for selective breeding, a group of London schoolchildren into evidence about whether Jewish migrants should be admitted, and the poorest stratum of the city into a hereditary "residuum." None of these steps was compelled by the arithmetic. After 1945 the grammar outlived the programme by uneven routes — Spearman's general factor, Burt's disputed twin correlations, the Pioneer Fund's money, *The Bell Curve*, and now commercial scores that rank embryos.

## 1. The Threepenny Laboratory: Science as Ideology at South Kensington

In the summer of 1884, visitors to the International Health Exhibition at South Kensington could, for threepence, learn exactly what kind of body they had. The Anthropometric Laboratory occupied a corridor fitted with instruments Galton had designed or adapted to measure the British public. You paid, submitted to seventeen tests — height, arm span, weight, strength of pull and squeeze, breathing power, swiftness of blow, visual acuity, colour sense, hearing, the highest audible note — and received a card printed with your results. What the pamphlet noted only in passing was that a duplicate was "preserved for statistical purposes."<sup aria-describedby="sn-1">1</sup>

<Sidenote number={1}>Galton, *On the Anthropometric Laboratory at the Late International Health Exhibition* (1884).</Sidenote>

One card went home as a souvenir. The other went into Galton's files.

By the end of the exhibition, "no less than 9,337" visitors had passed through. The tests were not a random selection of bodily attributes. They were chosen to capture traits Galton hoped would correlate with "natural ability," the quality he had been trying to pin down since *Hereditary Genius* fifteen years earlier; the sensory tests followed his bet that "the more perceptive the senses are of difference, the larger is the field upon which our judgment and intelligence can act."<sup aria-describedby="sn-2">2</sup> Visitors moved station by station in minutes. This was measurement designed for throughput, and James C. Scott's word for it is *legibility*: the reorganisation of complex lives into standard categories that can be centrally observed.<sup aria-describedby="sn-3">3</sup> Nine thousand particular bodies, each with its own history of nutrition, labour, and illness, entered the corridor and emerged as rows in a ledger that recorded measurements, not circumstances.

<Sidenote number={2}>Galton 1884; Galton, *Inquiries into Human Faculty and Its Development* (1883), p. 19.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={3}>Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998), pp. 2–3.</Sidenote>

### The duplicate card

This is the move a visitor could easily miss. Measurement produces a number; comparison places it against a chosen reference population; a hierarchy emerges only when someone treats the comparison as evidence of better or worse. A visitor who read a grip strength of forty-seven pounds understood it as a fact about themselves. To Galton it was one point in a distribution. Ian Hacking's argument that statistical categories bring new kinds of people into being clarifies what the duplicate accomplished: the cards made a reference distribution possible, and once an institution treats a distribution as a norm, it can identify deviations and attach claims to them.<sup aria-describedby="sn-4">4</sup> The normal curve does not say what its tails mean. Galton's programme did.

<Sidenote number={4}>Hacking, "Making Up People" (1986), pp. 231–32.</Sidenote>

Theodore Porter calls quantification a "technology of distance": numbers gain authority because they are impersonal.<sup aria-describedby="sn-5">5</sup> A dynamometer records grip strength regardless of who squeezes it, and the number carries no trace of the decision that made grip strength worth recording. By the time the numbers appeared in Galton's publications, read as evidence of hereditary pattern, they had passed through the authority of mechanical measurement. The laboratory's own findings were thin. Its contribution was structural: it showed that voluntary participation, mechanical measurement, and aggregation could yield a reference distribution. Donald MacKenzie's central argument is that Galton's and Pearson's statistical innovations were co-produced with the eugenic questions they addressed.<sup aria-describedby="sn-6">6</sup> The corridor makes that co-production visible: the instruments built the archive; the programme supplied the hereditary reading.

<Sidenote number={5}>Porter, *Trust in Numbers* (1995), p. ix.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={6}>MacKenzie, *Statistics in Britain, 1865–1930* (1981).</Sidenote>

## 2. From Quetelet's Average to Galton's Hierarchy

Before Galton, the normal curve belonged to Adolphe Quetelet. In 1835 the Belgian astronomer proposed a social physics: measure a human trait across a large enough population and the distribution would follow the bell curve astronomers used for observational error. Its mean was *l'homme moyen*, the average man, around whom individuals scattered with lawful regularity.<sup aria-describedby="sn-7">7</sup> The concept was descriptive. Quetelet wanted to explain why murder rates and marriage rates held steady from year to year; deviations were fluctuation around a signal. Alain Desrosières notes that his average man sometimes drifted into "a kind of prudent centrist, who avoided every conceivable form of excess," but Quetelet did not build a politics on the tails.<sup aria-describedby="sn-8">8</sup>

<Sidenote number={7}>Quetelet, *Sur l'homme et le développement de ses facultés, ou essai de physique sociale* (1835).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={8}>Desrosières, *The Politics of Large Numbers* (1998).</Sidenote>

Galton inherited the curve and performed a transformation that was mathematically slight and politically enormous. He made the average a failure. His word was "mediocrity." In *Hereditary Genius* (1869) the normal distribution is a grading system: the eminent above, the degenerate below, and in the middle the place ordinary people settle when nothing lifts them. Galton was explicit: "eminently gifted men are raised as much above mediocrity as idiots are depressed below it."<sup aria-describedby="sn-9">9</sup> The mathematical object was Quetelet's. A normal curve does not tell us whether its tails are pathological. The curve is a shape; pathology is a decision.

<Sidenote number={9}>Galton, *Hereditary Genius* (1869), p. 36.</Sidenote>

### Counting privilege

*Hereditary Genius* drew its "eminent" men from biographical dictionaries, legal records, lists of Royal Society fellows, senior clergy, commanders, and statesmen, then counted how often they had eminent relatives. Eminence clustered in families more than chance predicted, and Galton inferred that natural ability was inherited. Stephen Jay Gould named the two errors: *reification*, treating eminence — a designation conferred by institutions — as a measurable property of individuals; and *ranking*, arranging people on a single scale and letting the curve make the scale look natural.<sup aria-describedby="sn-10">10</sup> The sample was built by its conclusion. A man became a judge because his father was a barrister and his grandfather could afford to educate his sons; to call this hereditary genius is to mistake the social reproduction of a class for the biological transmission of a trait.

<Sidenote number={10}>Gould, *The Mismeasure of Man* (1981).</Sidenote>

The dataset was also entirely male, because its categories of eminence were closed to Victorian women. Women appear in the pedigree tables only as the mothers, wives, and daughters through whom eminent lineages pass. Galton's 1897 paper on the average contribution of each ancestor treated maternal and paternal inheritance symmetrically in its algebra.<sup aria-describedby="sn-11">11</sup> The algebra was gender-neutral; the data was not. Women were necessary to the equations as transmitters of hereditary value and absent from the evidence as bearers of their own ability.

<Sidenote number={11}>Galton, "The Average Contribution of each several Ancestor to the total Heritage of the Offspring," *Proceedings of the Royal Society* (1897).</Sidenote>

### Distance from the mean

The modern z-score makes the distinction concrete, though Galton never used the term. A test score of 85, in a reference group with mean 70 and standard deviation 10, has a z-score of $(85 - 70)/10 = 1.5$ — roughly the ninety-third percentile if the distribution is about normal. That is a standardised distance, not a rank in itself. It becomes a ranking only when an institution chooses the reference population and treats relative position as evidence of ability, need, or worth. Mathematical form also made the claim portable: eugenic institutions later carried Galton's vocabulary into pedigree work at the Eugenics Record Office at Cold Spring Harbor, immigration restriction campaigns, and American sterilisation policy — though each required its own funding, law, and politics, not an automatic chain of causation from 1869.<sup aria-describedby="sn-12">12</sup> The form travels. Institutions decide what it will authorise.

<Sidenote number={12}>Kevles, *In the Name of Eugenics* (1995).</Sidenote>

## 3. Regression as Destiny: The Mathematical Case for Selective Breeding

Galton's most consequential discovery began with sweet peas. In the late 1870s he sent packets of seeds of different sizes to friends, asked them to grow the plants, and measured the seeds they returned. Large seeds produced offspring that were larger than average but not as large as their parents; small seeds produced small offspring, less extremely so. He called this "regression toward mediocrity," and found the same pattern in human height. Plot parent against offspring and the fitted line slopes towards the mean with a slope less than one.

The finding is genuine. Regression to the mean is a statistical phenomenon, not a biological one: it appears in any paired measurement whose correlation is less than perfect. A student who scores exceptionally well on one exam will, on average, score less exceptionally on the next; a city with an unusually high crime rate one year will, on average, have a lower rate the next, whether or not any policy changed. Galton read it as a warning. Without selection, the gains of one generation would be diluted in the next; if the superior bred with one another, the right tail would thicken. In *Inquiries into Human Faculty* (1883) he coined the word "eugenics" for the programme that followed.<sup aria-describedby="sn-13">13</sup> The mathematical justification was regression. The political programme was breeding. Nothing in the slope of a parent–offspring line says the extremes ought to be preserved. The lament was not in the data. It was in the reader.

<Sidenote number={13}>Galton 1883.</Sidenote>

### Pearson's formalisation

Karl Pearson gave Galton's graphical work an algebraic form. His 1896 memoir developed general treatments of regression and the product-moment correlation coefficient *r*, and called the correlation of parent and offspring "the mathematical measure of heredity."<sup aria-describedby="sn-14">14</sup> The coefficient runs from −1 to +1. A correlation of 0.5 between parent and offspring height means taller parents tend to have taller children while leaving much of the variation unexplained. It cannot say why — genes, environment, or social circumstance. It is a one-number summary of linear association, not a proof of mechanism. The historical question is why the first extended formal treatment made hereditary resemblance its central problem.

<Sidenote number={14}>Pearson, "Mathematical Contributions to the Theory of Evolution. III. Regression, Heredity, and Panmixia," *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A* (1896), pp. 255, 259, 265.</Sidenote>

### Alice Lee

Pearson's Biometric Laboratory at University College London, and *Biometrika* from 1901, turned family relations into an apparatus for estimating hereditary transmission; in the 1903 tables he compiled with Alice Lee, mothers' measurements mattered because they entered a calculation of descent.<sup aria-describedby="sn-15">15</sup> Lee's own 1901 study complicates any simple account of the laboratory's gendered division of labour. The paper was hers — Pearson wrote that its reductions and inferences were "substantially her work." Measuring living anatomists, University College staff, and women of Bedford College, she tested the claim that skull capacity tracked intellectual power, and found "no marked correlation" in either sex.<sup aria-describedby="sn-16">16</sup> The result did not end craniometry. It showed that the same apparatus could disconfirm a claim that animated it — and that which relationships to measure, and what to conclude, remained political choices. That question carries over to every correlation put before an institution today, from air pollution and asthma to benefit sanctions and employment: what does this one-number summary make visible, what does it conceal, and what decision is about to be made from it?

<Sidenote number={15}>Pearson and Lee, "On the Laws of Inheritance in Man: I. Inheritance of Physical Characters," *Biometrika* (1903).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={16}>Lee, "Data for the Problem of Evolution in Man. VI. A First Study of the Correlation of the Human Skull," *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A* (1901), pp. 225, 255–59.</Sidenote>

## 4. The Colonial Ledger: Ranking at Imperial Scale

Eugenic thought also supplied a language for ranking groups against one another — who was fit to govern, to labour, to settle, or to be excluded. In 1873 Galton published a letter in *The Times* titled "Africa for the Chinese," proposing that British policy encourage Chinese settlement on the East African coast, where settlers would "supplant the inferior Negro race."<sup aria-describedby="sn-17">17</sup> The letter is advocacy, not a statistical study. It matters because it makes the hierarchy behind the later measurements explicit: two peoples become entries in an imperial calculation, and the displacement of one by the other is entered as an improvement in the account.

<Sidenote number={17}>Galton, "Africa for the Chinese," *The Times*, 5 June 1873.</Sidenote>

### When the imperial grammar came home

Pearson and Margaret Moul's 1925 study of Russian and Polish Jewish children in London asked a metropolitan question: whether Britain should permit what the authors called indiscriminate immigration, and whether migrants should meet standards of physique and mentality — a problem they framed as "national eugenics."<sup aria-describedby="sn-18">18</sup> They assembled dossiers on about 600 boys and nearly as many girls at the Jews' Free School in Aldgate, combining school, medical, and home-visitor records and teachers' ratings of intelligence on a scale the article presented as a measure of innate mental power. The children were classified as "alien" even though, by the paper's own count, 71.7 per cent had been born in England.<sup aria-describedby="sn-19">19</sup>

<Sidenote number={18}>Pearson and Moul, "The Problem of Alien Immigration into Great Britain, Illustrated by an Examination of Russian and Polish Jewish Children," Part I, *Annals of Eugenics* 1(1) (1925), pp. 5–8.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={19}>Pearson and Moul 1925, Part I, pp. 9–11.</Sidenote>

The study made comparisons — with London County Council schools in good and poor districts and with material from Leeds — but its framework decided what counted as an explanation. When Jewish children in London and Leeds showed similar growth patterns, the authors read it as evidence against Franz Boas's account of environmental change and for racial growth curves. Part I ends by saying a favourable judgment could follow only if the children proved markedly superior in intelligence to native Gentiles.<sup aria-describedby="sn-20">20</sup> The standard was set before the data were reported: admission had to be earned by superiority. Part II supplied the verdict: "Taken on the average, and regarding both sexes, this alien Jewish population is somewhat inferior physically and mentally to the native population."<sup aria-describedby="sn-21">21</sup>

<Sidenote number={20}>Pearson and Moul 1925, Part I, pp. 25–28, 51.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={21}>Pearson and Moul 1925, Part II, *Annals of Eugenics* 1(2), pp. 125–26.</Sidenote>

The authors wrote that the immigration question could be settled only in the "cold light of statistical inquiry," and that the Galton Laboratory had no political, religious, or social prejudice.<sup aria-describedby="sn-22">22</sup> In Porter's terms the political decision was not erased but relocated: the choice to make physique, mentality, and presumed heredity into entry criteria was made before any calculation, and once the question took statistical form, argument was channelled towards samples and coefficients. Tables can record patterns. They cannot decide whether a political community should attach rights and belonging to them.

<Sidenote number={22}>Pearson and Moul 1925, Part I, p. 8.</Sidenote>

## 5. The Residuum: When Statistics Meets the Poor Law

Galton's *Essays in Eugenics* (1909) contains a small act of translation. He took Charles Booth's Class A from the survey of East London and placed it in "the lowermost third of our 'v and below'."<sup aria-describedby="sn-23">23</sup> A category of urban insecurity had become a location in a hereditary distribution. Booth had called Class A "the lowest class" and "now hereditary to a very considerable extent," but his surrounding pages treat it as shaped by economic conditions, charity, and movement between classes.<sup aria-describedby="sn-24">24</sup> Galton supplied the eugenic reading, and eugenic writers recast the *residuum* as constitutionally different from the respectable working class: poor for lack of hereditary capacity, with children who would be poor for the same reason.

<Sidenote number={23}>Galton, *Essays in Eugenics* (1909), p. 20.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={24}>Booth, *Life and Labour of the People* (1889), pp. 37–38.</Sidenote>

Under the old Poor Law, the line between deserving and undeserving was drawn by people — magistrates and relieving officers who saw applicants face to face. Their judgments were inconsistent and contestable, and that was also the system's humanity: an inconsistent system can be argued with. Eugenic writers proposed to stabilise it through classification. If the residuum was hereditary, the old moral distinction became a matter of data, and the relieving officer's judgment, with its capacity for mercy, could be subordinated to a statistician's ranking. If the left tail was biological, redistribution was futile.

That vocabulary entered the debates around the 1909 Royal Commission on the Poor Laws, though neither its Majority nor its Minority Report was a eugenic programme. The Mental Deficiency Act 1913 created four statutory classes — "idiots," "imbeciles," "feeble-minded persons," and "moral imbeciles" — and enabled institutional placement and supervision.<sup aria-describedby="sn-25">25</sup> In 1929 the Wood Committee described a "subnormal or social problem group, representing approximately 10 per cent of the whole population," and drew the hereditary inference at once: "the dull children of the present generation … are the potential parents of many feeble-minded in the next generation."<sup aria-describedby="sn-26">26</sup> The percentage did the work: it turned a contested category into a population object that could be counted, costed, and administered. Later categories — Keith Joseph's 1972 "cycle of deprivation," the "underclass" — are historically distinct and should not be collapsed into eugenic language.<sup aria-describedby="sn-27">27</sup> But each can turn poverty into a property of a group. A relieving officer's judgment could be challenged by a neighbour's testimony. A position in a ranked distribution can be challenged only by contesting the category, its evidence, and its calculation — resources those assigned to its left tail seldom possess.

<Sidenote number={25}>Mental Deficiency Act 1913, ss. 1–3, 30.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={26}>Board of Education and Board of Control, *Report of the Mental Deficiency Committee* (Wood Report, 1929), p. 93.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={27}>Joseph, speech to the Pre-School Playgroups Association, 29 June 1972.</Sidenote>

## 6. The Ledger's Long Life: From *Biometrika* to *The Bell Curve*

The Second World War discredited eugenic policy and racial science. By then the statistical repertoire formed around them had travelled beyond explicit eugenics. The connections that follow are of different kinds, and conflating them would flatten the history.

**Spearman's *g*.** In 1904 Charles Spearman looked at the positive correlations among children's scores on different tests and proposed a common function, provisionally called general intelligence.<sup aria-describedby="sn-28">28</sup> This was mathematical psychology, not eugenic policy, but it was a move of compression: several scores, a common factor, a rank carrying a claim about a person. Gould names the danger as reification — treating a statistical abstraction as a unitary biological property.<sup aria-describedby="sn-29">29</sup>

<Sidenote number={28}>Spearman, "'General Intelligence,' Objectively Determined and Measured," *American Journal of Psychology* (1904), pp. 272–73.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={29}>Gould 1981.</Sidenote>

**Burt under audit.** Cyril Burt held an influential place in the British educational psychology that shaped selective schooling, though he did not design the 11-plus alone.<sup aria-describedby="sn-30">30</sup> In 1966 he reported correlations for identical twins reared apart of .771, .883, and .874, while conceding that what he had calculated was "a somewhat artificial abstraction."<sup aria-describedby="sn-31">31</sup> Leon Kamin's criticism, L. S. Hearnshaw's biography, and N. J. Mackintosh's re-examination leave the data a contested object rather than a foundation.<sup aria-describedby="sn-32">32</sup> A coefficient to three decimal places cannot substitute for traceable data and methods; where tests assign children's futures, access to those records is part of the politics of measurement.

<Sidenote number={30}>Wooldridge, *Measuring the Mind* (1994).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={31}>Burt, "The Genetic Determination of Differences in Intelligence," *British Journal of Psychology* (1966), pp. 139, 147.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={32}>Kamin, *The Science and Politics of I.Q.* (1974); Hearnshaw, *Cyril Burt, Psychologist* (1979); Mackintosh (ed.), *Cyril Burt: Fraud or Framed?* (1995).</Sidenote>

**Private money and public restatement.** The Pioneer Fund, incorporated by Wickliffe Preston Draper in 1937 with a charter rooted in heredity and "race betterment," later supported Arthur Jensen, J. Philippe Rushton, and Richard Lynn.<sup aria-describedby="sn-33">33</sup> Its importance is institutional: funding can keep a programme alive after its claims have lost wider legitimacy. Herrnstein and Murray's *The Bell Curve* (1994) returned intelligence, hierarchy, and social policy to a mass audience, drawing on a repertoire of standardised distributions, heritability claims, and factor models with historical links to Galton, Pearson, and Spearman; Claude Fischer and his co-authors challenged its construction of intelligence, its racial categories, and its causal inferences.<sup aria-describedby="sn-34">34</sup> A distribution and a statistical appendix can give a political argument the look of technical finality.

<Sidenote number={33}>Tucker, *The Funding of Scientific Racism: Wickliffe Draper and the Pioneer Fund* (2002).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={34}>Herrnstein and Murray, *The Bell Curve* (1994); Fischer, Hout, Jankowski et al., *Inequality by Design: Cracking the Bell Curve Myth* (1996).</Sidenote>

**The ledger continues.** Polygenic embryo testing (PGT-P) aggregates many small genetic associations into a probabilistic score. One provider, Herasight, markets embryo scores for disease risk and presents intelligence among the traits such testing can address.<sup aria-describedby="sn-35">35</sup> The American Society for Reproductive Medicine's committees describe PGT-P as unproven, say it should not be offered clinically, and reject its use for non-medical traits; in the United Kingdom, the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority has confirmed that it is not lawful.<sup aria-describedby="sn-36">36</sup> State eugenics worked through law and institutions; commercial PGT-P works through clinical markets and parental choice. The difference matters. The comparison asks what happens when a statistical prediction is invited to stand for a future person's prospects — not whether every parent or geneticist is an heir to Galton. In each of these cases a distribution becomes politically consequential through the work assigned to it, the categories selected for it, and the futures that become easier to administer once a rank has been given.

<Sidenote number={35}>Herasight, "What Is PGT-P? Polygenic Embryo Screening Explained" (18 February 2026).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={36}>Klipstein et al., "Use of Preimplantation Genetic Testing for Polygenic Disorders (PGT-P): An Ethics Committee Opinion," *Fertility and Sterility* 125 (2026), pp. 24–30; HFEA, "PGT-P is not lawful in the UK" (2025).</Sidenote>

### Connection Forward

Chapter 3 follows the actuarial revolution — the moment when the welfare state replaced the Poor Law's moral assessment with the contribution record and the eligibility threshold. Where the eugenic tradition ranked populations and drew policy conclusions from the rank, the actuarial tradition classified them and derived entitlements from the classification. Both operations translated political decisions into mathematical form; the political decisions they embedded were different, and so were the exclusions they produced.
