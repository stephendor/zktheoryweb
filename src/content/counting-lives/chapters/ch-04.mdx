## Synopsis

In the late 1960s two welfare mathematics were available at once. One was administrative: a single communicable number, indexed and defensible, that needed to know nothing specific about the households it described. The other was built from grocery receipts, fuel bills, and household budgets: Johnnie Tillmon and the National Welfare Rights Organization pricing what families needed, Brian Abel-Smith and Peter Townsend using the government's own data to show that poverty had survived the welfare state, Townsend measuring deprivation item by item, and in Scotland a tradition of costed housing politics running back to the 1915 Glasgow rent strike. The second was closer to how households lived. It lost — politically, as the pressure of the late 1960s ebbed, and intellectually much earlier, when Margaret Reid's household economics was classified out of the discipline that would train welfare-state economists. Where the grocery-list tradition survived, it survived simplified: Townsend's index became a percentage of median income, as Orshansky's matrix became an indexed line. The cost of that defeat is visible in Universal Credit, in rules drawn without the household's view of them — and in the food bank records that now do the counting the state chose not to do.

## 1. Two Mathematics, One Moment

In the late 1960s, two things were true about poverty measurement in the United States and Britain that would not stay true together for long. The administrative welfare state had reached its postwar maturity: it had learned to count its poor efficiently, consistently, and at scale, in a form that could be defended in a congressional hearing and printed in a newspaper. And at the same moment, the most sustained challenge to that administrative mathematics was at the height of its reach. Johnnie Tillmon and the National Welfare Rights Organization were confronting federal officials with itemised household budgets priced from grocery receipts and utility bills. Brian Abel-Smith and Peter Townsend had used the British government's own expenditure data to show that poverty had survived the welfare state, and the Child Poverty Action Group had been founded on that finding. Townsend was in the field with a survey of some two thousand households in fifty-one parliamentary constituencies, building a sixty-item index of deprivation. Mollie Orshansky's household-specific thresholds were in official use.

Each of these was mathematically disciplined. NWRO chapters priced actual food in actual stores for specific family types in specific cities. Townsend measured diet, clothing, housing, and social participation as well as income. Orshansky built 124 thresholds from food plans and survey ratios. Each required more data and more local knowledge to maintain than the administrative model it contested. Each was closer to how households lived. None became the basis on which benefits were set.

What separated the two traditions was not technical quality but commitment. The administrative model's commitment — visible in the Council of Economic Advisers' single \$3,000 line of 1963 and in the move to CPI indexing in 1969 — was that a welfare mathematics must above all be administrable: trackable over time, communicable to the press, usable without knowing anything specific about the households it described. The grocery-list tradition's commitment was the opposite: that honesty requires specificity about household composition, local costs, and what families actually had to buy. Theodore Porter explains which commitment wins. Numbers gain institutional authority not because they are the most accurate description of what they measure but because they are impersonal — because they appear to come from a procedure rather than a decision.<sup aria-describedby="sn-1">1</sup> James Scott names what is lost: the practical, situated knowledge he calls *mētis*, in this case the knowledge of how particular households in particular configurations actually got by.<sup aria-describedby="sn-2">2</sup>

<Sidenote number={1}>Porter, *Trust in Numbers* (1995), p. 8.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={2}>Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998).</Sidenote>

But the defeat of the grocery-list tradition was not primarily an epistemological event. It was political. Frances Fox Piven and Richard Cloward's account of welfare history describes a cycle: relief expands when disorder makes inadequate provision politically costly, and contracts when the disorder subsides and the labour market again wants a precarious workforce.<sup aria-describedby="sn-3">3</sup> The grocery-list tradition's peak, roughly 1965 to 1969, coincided with the peak of urban unrest and of pressure for welfare expansion. As that pressure ebbed, the coalition that had briefly made an adequate income thinkable fractured, and the administrative machinery was consolidated around simpler numbers.

<Sidenote number={3}>Piven and Cloward, *Regulating the Poor* (1971).</Sidenote>

That changes what kind of story this is. The grocery-list tradition was not defeated by being wrong. Later measurement has repeatedly moved back towards it: the US Supplemental Poverty Measure prices food, clothing, shelter, and utilities and adjusts for local housing costs; Townsend's relative deprivation framework became the basis of European and Scottish poverty statistics. This chapter traces the defeat in four registers: Tillmon's budgets, Townsend's Britain, Margaret Reid's excluded discipline, and Scotland's specific costs.

## 2. Johnnie Tillmon's Budget: Welfare as a Women's Issue

Johnnie Tillmon was born in Scott, Arkansas, in 1926, the daughter of sharecroppers, and moved to the Nickerson Gardens housing project in Watts, Los Angeles. When illness stopped her working in a laundry, she went on welfare, and in 1963 she organised Aid to Needy Children Mothers Anonymous at Nickerson Gardens, one of the first welfare mothers' organisations in the country. It affiliated with the new National Welfare Rights Organization, which Tillmon would later lead. The NWRO's chapters had often begun as women around kitchen tables comparing their benefit notices with their grocery receipts and arriving at the same arithmetic.

The fullest statement of what that arithmetic demanded is Tillmon's essay "Welfare Is a Women's Issue," in the first issue of *Ms.* in 1972.<sup aria-describedby="sn-4">4</sup> It opens with a self-inventory — a woman, a Black woman, a poor woman, a fat woman, a middle-aged woman, on welfare — and then its consequence: if you are all those things, "you don't count at all, except as a statistic." The move is deliberate. Welfare recipients appeared in the administrative apparatus as cases, costs, and aggregate rates; in Tillmon's essay they appear as women with specific expenses, specific work, and a government-defined income that does not meet them. Its central claim is that welfare mothers already work — raising children, producing the household, and navigating the welfare bureaucracy itself — and that this labour goes unrecognised and unpaid, while the rules on earned income made paid work financially punishing. Welfare, she wrote, was like a "super-sexist marriage."

<Sidenote number={4}>Tillmon, "Welfare Is a Women's Issue," *Ms.* (Spring 1972).</Sidenote>

The NWRO turned the gap into a demand. Its Guaranteed Adequate Income was budget-anchored: \$5,500 a year for a family of four in 1968–69, raised to \$6,500 in 1970, derived from the Department of Labor's adequacy budgets and from household price surveys that chapters carried out in their own markets. Methodologically this was Orshansky's approach transposed into organising: start from what households need to buy, price it, and derive the benefit from the arithmetic rather than the fiscal calendar. Against it, the Nixon administration's Family Assistance Plan proposed a guarantee of \$1,600 for a family of four, and the NWRO refused to endorse it on precisely those grounds.<sup aria-describedby="sn-5">5</sup>

<Sidenote number={5}>Piven and Cloward 1971.</Sidenote>

That the method was right and the politics were defeated needs a different kind of explanation than methodological failure. bell hooks, in *Where We Stand*, argues that class is more systematically silenced in American public life than race or gender, because naming it threatens capitalist ideology directly.<sup aria-describedby="sn-6">6</sup> The NWRO's arithmetic was produced by Black women at the intersection of that class silence, racial exclusion, and the devaluation of reproductive knowledge. What they had was direct, material expertise in the cost of survival. What they were, in the categories that governed whose arithmetic was consulted, was welfare recipients — a category built to exclude its subjects from deliberation. hooks' account of how mainstream second-wave feminism centred the aspirations of women already positioned to benefit from equal-pay and career politics fits Tillmon's essay exactly: for most women on welfare, liberation could not be separated from income, and an adequate income had a dollar figure that could be checked against a grocery receipt. Dorothy Height and the National Council of Negro Women pursued the same commitment by a different route in the mid-1960s, building programmes in Mississippi from Black women's own accounts of what their households needed. The federal apparatus received such work as community programmes or social work — outside the genealogy of poverty *research*, and so outside the infrastructure that set benefit rates.

<Sidenote number={6}>hooks, *Where We Stand: Class Matters* (2000).</Sidenote>

The political conditions shifted under them. The Family Assistance Plan failed in Congress, the NWRO lost the disruptive leverage that had been its main source of power, and by 1975 the organisation had collapsed. Tillmon died in 1995, a year before the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act abolished AFDC and replaced it with TANF, whose work requirements and time limits were the instruments she had described in 1972. The arithmetic had not changed. The conditions under which it could be heard had been removed.

## 3. Townsend's Britain: Relative Deprivation Against Flat Rates

In December 1965, Brian Abel-Smith and Peter Townsend published *The Poor and the Poorest*, a short pamphlet built from the Ministry of Labour's own expenditure surveys. Its finding was explosive in Harold Wilson's Britain: poverty had not been abolished by the Beveridge welfare state. By their measure the share of households in poverty had risen from about 10 per cent in 1953–54 to about 18 per cent in 1960 — from some four million people to roughly seven and a half million.<sup aria-describedby="sn-7">7</sup> The Child Poverty Action Group, which had been meeting at Toynbee Hall since March, made the finding the basis of a campaign.

<Sidenote number={7}>Abel-Smith and Townsend, *The Poor and the Poorest* (1965).</Sidenote>

Their method was the British version of Orshansky's commitment, developed independently. They defined poverty as living below 140 per cent of the National Assistance scale rate appropriate to the household, plus actual housing costs. The margin was not arbitrary: bare National Assistance left households unable to meet irregular expenses or replace worn-out things. Like Orshansky multiplying food costs by three, they took the state's own minimum as the foundation and showed that an honest standard built on it was still above what the state actually paid. The indictment was internal to the system's own premises — and it came from data the Ministry had collected to weight a price index, not to find the poor.

Townsend's more ambitious attempt to make material specificity the basis of measurement was the survey for *Poverty in the United Kingdom*, carried out in 1968–69 and published in 1979. Its deprivation index asked what households had and did without: a cooked breakfast most days, a Sunday joint, a week's holiday away from home, children's birthday parties, a refrigerator, a home without damp.<sup aria-describedby="sn-8">8</sup> The index did not measure luxury. It measured relative deprivation — the inability to take part in the activities and customs a society treats as ordinary. Townsend looked for a point in the income distribution below which deprivation rose sharply, and proposed it as an objective basis for a poverty line grounded in how people lived rather than in what the state had decided to pay. The argument was the same as in 1965: the official measure administers a threshold, and a threshold is not the same thing as poverty. When the threshold is set below what a grounded measure requires, the apparatus will describe as non-poor households that are poor. That is not a measurement error. It is a political choice in actuarial form.

<Sidenote number={8}>Townsend, *Poverty in the United Kingdom* (1979), esp. pp. 249–61.</Sidenote>

CPAG, under Frank Field from 1969, turned the argument into a sustained campaign against the Wilson, Heath, and Callaghan governments, and the welfare rights networks Tony Lynes helped build taught claimants to close the gap between what the system said and what it delivered. In the 1980s the argument met a government that rejected relative poverty outright, on the ground that a relative measure can never fall if all incomes rise. In 1988 the Department of Social Security replaced its low-income statistics with the *Households Below Average Income* series, measuring position in the income distribution without calling it poverty. The administrative model that had won in the United States in the late 1960s won the same argument in Britain by a different route.

Townsend's framework survived institutionally because it had entered European poverty measurement more deeply than the British welfare state's own categories. The threshold that now dominates European and UK statistics — 60 per cent of median equivalised household income — is Townsend's social-participation argument as a single number. It is, in that sense, a simplified Townsend, as the official US line is a simplified Orshansky: the frame preserved, the instrument that generated it discarded. In both cases what was lost was the measure's capacity to be contested by the people it described, because its elements were specific enough to check against a receipt, a fuel bill, a birthday cake. An income position, however well placed, tells a household nothing about which inadequacies are driving its poverty. The grocery list, converted into a percentile, can no longer be priced.

## 4. Margaret Reid's Unreceived Inheritance

In 1965 Gary Becker published "A Theory of the Allocation of Time," arguing that households are producers as well as consumers, combining purchased goods with their own time to make what they actually live on. It became one of the most cited papers in economics, and central to the Nobel Prize he received in 1992.

Margaret G. Reid had made essentially the same argument in 1934.

Reid's *Economics of Household Production* treated the unpaid work of the household — cooking, laundering, childcare, managing the material infrastructure of domestic life — as economically productive. Her test was whether an activity could be handed to a paid worker: household production, she wrote, consists of "those unpaid activities" that "might be replaced by market goods, or paid services."<sup aria-describedby="sn-9">9</sup> By that test a mother preparing meals was doing productive work, and its exclusion from national accounts was an artefact of where the boundary had been drawn, not a conceptual necessity. The difference between Reid's reception and Becker's was not analytical content but institutional position. Becker wrote from an economics department in the idiom of formal microeconomic theory. Reid, trained under Hazel Kyrk, whose *A Theory of Consumption* (1923) had treated consumption as a social and material practice, wrote from within home economics — a field the economics profession had spent decades classifying as vocational and feminised.

<Sidenote number={9}>Reid, *Economics of Household Production* (1934), p. 11.</Sidenote>

What that tradition could see was exactly what the grocery-list tradition needed. A welfare economics built on Reid's framework would have had to ask, for any benefit level, whether a household of a given composition could actually produce from that income the meals, warmth, and care subsistence requires — and to price the domestic labour of turning a food budget into dinners. It would have looked a good deal like Orshansky's food-plan matrices or the NWRO's priced budgets.

Three mechanisms kept it out of the welfare state's governing mathematics. The Smith-Hughes Act of 1917 funded home economics as a vocational subject alongside agriculture and trades; money flowed to teaching cookery and domestic management, not to the research programme Kyrk and Reid were building, and over time departments were renamed — family studies, human ecology, consumer science — in a retreat from any claim to economics. Postwar economics then professionalised around formal theory, econometrics, and labour-market analysis, drawing sharp boundaries that left time-use and domestic-labour research outside; the gender composition of the two fields made the boundary a hierarchy, and judgments of rigour followed from it rather than producing it. Finally, the economists who designed and ran the welfare state — at the Council of Economic Advisers, in the social security actuarial offices — were trained in a curriculum from which household production theory had been removed. For officials who preferred a single \$3,000 threshold, administrative convenience and the limits of their training were the same thing.

This is the chapter's deepest layer. The political defeats of the late 1960s were prepared by an earlier intellectual one: the reclassification of the research programme that would have supplied the grocery-list tradition's foundations as feminised, vocational, and not economics. By the time Orshansky was building her thresholds, that tradition had already been stripped from the training of the people who would decide whether to adopt them. hooks' account of the silencing of class has a narrower analogue here: the refusal to treat the work of maintaining life — feeding, clothing, sheltering, and caring for particular bodies — as economic work that belongs inside the discipline that sets benefit rates. Reid built the toolkit that would have made the arithmetic of survival unavoidable. Its exclusion was, in effect, a choice about what welfare mathematics would be allowed to see.

## 5. Scotland's Specific Costs: The Grocery List in the Highlands

On 17 November 1915, Glasgow's rent strike reached the courts. Crowds of several thousand gathered outside the sheriff court, with shipyard and munitions workers threatening to stop work, as tenants were prosecuted for refusing rent increases. The prosecutions collapsed, and within weeks Parliament passed the Increase of Rent and Mortgage Interest (War Restrictions) Act, freezing rents at prewar levels. The strike had been organised by working-class women — Mary Barbour in Govan, Helen Crawfurd, Mary Laird, Jessie Stephen — through the Glasgow Women's Housing Association, which had spent the previous year documenting rent increases, disrepair, and evictions case by case, including evictions of soldiers' wives whose husbands were at the front. John Wheatley's 1913 pamphlet *Eight Pound Cottages for Glasgow Citizens* had already set out, from the city's own finances, a costed plan for decent working-class housing. As Minister of Health in 1924 he turned a specific cost calculation into statute: the Housing Act's long-term subsidies, set to produce rents low-paid workers could afford, built over half a million council homes. The grocery-list tradition in Scotland had a legislative record before the welfare state existed.

The Scotland the postwar welfare state inherited was materially specific in ways Beveridge's flat rates were not designed to see: stone-built tenements that were expensive to heat, peripheral estates built quickly to poor thermal standards, mining and fishing communities set apart from the populations on whose averages National Assistance scales were set in 1948. *Born to Fail?*, the National Children's Bureau study published in 1973 from the National Child Development Study, put the divergence in numbers: across Britain one child in sixteen was severely disadvantaged on a combined measure of family structure, housing, and income; in Scotland, one in ten.<sup aria-describedby="sn-10">10</sup>

<Sidenote number={10}>Wedge and Prosser, *Born to Fail?* (National Children's Bureau, 1973).</Sidenote>

### Heat, food, damp

Three cost differences make Scotland's case structurally different from England's rather than simply more deprived. The first is heat. Scotland is colder, its heating season longer, and a large share of rural homes are off the gas grid, heated by oil, electricity, or solid fuel at higher unit costs. In 2024, 28.7 per cent of Scottish households — about 732,000 — were in fuel poverty, and the median fuel-poor household needed another £1,030 a year to reach adequate warmth.<sup aria-describedby="sn-11">11</sup> A benefit calibrated to UK-wide average heating costs is set for a country these households do not live in.

<Sidenote number={11}>Scottish Government, *Scottish House Condition Survey: 2024 Key Findings*. The 2023 figures were 33.9 per cent and £1,250; the fall is attributed mainly to lower energy prices.</Sidenote>

The second is food. Studies of shelf prices in remote Scottish shops have found premiums of between 10 and 40 per cent over urban supermarkets, depending on the basket and the store. Research at Scotland's Rural College using actual household purchases finds a much smaller average premium — because around 42 per cent of households in remote areas "out-shop," travelling to cheaper supermarkets.<sup aria-describedby="sn-12">12</sup> The premium does not disappear; it is converted into a transport cost on the same budget. For households without a car — disproportionately older, disabled, and the poorest — local prices are the only prices.

<Sidenote number={12}>Scotland's Rural College (SRUC), studies of food prices and out-shopping in Scotland's remote areas, including "Food Prices in Scottish Remote Rural Areas: Measuring and Explaining the 'Remoteness Premium'" and the COVID-19 natural-experiment study in *Food Security* (2024).</Sidenote>

The third is damp. Solid-walled tenements and poorly built postwar estates have long suffered damp that is hard to remedy and expensive to heat away. A damp home costs more to keep at a given temperature, and its consequences — mould, respiratory illness, missed school — generate costs that appear nowhere in the benefit calculation meant to prevent them.

### A cost matrix in statute

Scotland's Fuel Poverty (Targets, Definition and Strategy) Act 2019 is the clearest case in British policy of the grocery-list epistemology written into law. It defines fuel poverty not as a position in the national income distribution but as the condition of a household that cannot afford adequate warmth after its other essential costs and still remain above a Minimum Income Standard — with uplifts for remote rural and island areas. That requires measuring what a specific household's heating actually costs, at the prices it actually faces, in the climate it actually lives in. It is a location-specific cost matrix, structurally analogous to Orshansky's household-specific one: the same insight applied to geography that she applied to household composition. A household in Stornoway needs a different income from one in Sheffield to reach the same standard of living, for reasons that can be measured and openly contested. The Scottish measure says so in statute. Universal Credit does not.

The women who documented rent books in Govan in 1915 and the researchers who price food baskets on the islands now make the same claim: poverty is specific, the specificity is measurable, and a governance mathematics that averages it away has made a choice about what it will see.

## 6. The Defeat and Its Cost: What Administrative Abstraction Cannot See

The administrative tradition did, for a time, maintain its own household-needs measure. The Bureau of Labor Statistics priced a City Worker's Family Budget from 1947, and by the late 1960s published lower, moderate, and higher budgets for a family of four in cities across the United States, built from USDA food plans and household expenditure surveys and adjusted for local climate and transport. Its moderate budget sat far above the poverty threshold, and the families between the two — below a decent standard but above the poverty line — numbered in the millions. The poverty line was not built to count them. The family budget could have been, had it been treated as a policy target rather than a statistical exercise. In 1980 an expert committee chaired by the Wisconsin economist Harold Watts proposed replacing the item-by-item market basket with a standard tied to the distribution of household spending — one distributional judgment in place of several hundred item-level judgments that could each be checked at the till.<sup aria-describedby="sn-13">13</sup> In 1981 the programme was discontinued. Its fate and Orshansky's followed the same logic: the aggregate over the specific, the position in the national distribution over the actual cost of a winter coat in Minneapolis.

<Sidenote number={13}>Watts, report of the Expert Committee on Family Budget Revisions, *Monthly Labor Review* (December 1980).</Sidenote>

The cost of that defeat is not historical. It is visible in the structure of Universal Credit.

### The taper

Universal Credit withdraws 55 pence for every pound of net earnings above a work allowance. Once earnings cross the income tax and National Insurance thresholds, three withdrawals act on the same pound: 20 pence in income tax, 8 pence in National Insurance, and 55 per cent of the remaining 72 pence — 39.6 pence — from Universal Credit. The household keeps 32.4 pence. Its effective marginal tax rate is 67.6 per cent, higher than the top rate of income tax. Each withdrawal was set by its own rules for its own purposes; none was set from the position of a household sitting inside all of them at once. Orshansky would have started from the household receipt, and the gradient would have been visible before the first rule was written.

### The hard zeros

Some rules were not gradients but discontinuities. From April 2017 the two-child limit removed the child element of Universal Credit and Child Tax Credit for third and subsequent children: a hard zero in the formula for a child the household's grocery receipt continued to register in every nappy, school dinner, and winter coat. Its cost was calculable to the penny from published rates, and the Child Poverty Action Group and the Institute for Fiscal Studies calculated it; the government's own estimate of the poverty it caused arrived only when it set out to abolish it. In England, free school meals for children in Universal Credit households stopped at an earnings threshold of £7,400 a year, so a small pay rise could cost a family more than it gained.

Both have now gone. The Universal Credit (Removal of Two Child Limit) Act 2026 received Royal Assent on 18 March 2026 and took effect in April; from September 2026, every child in a Universal Credit household in England qualifies for free school meals.<sup aria-describedby="sn-14">14</sup> The removals are the clearest evidence that the rules were political choices. Parliament wrote a zero for the third child in 2016 and removed it a decade later; the government drew an earnings cliff and then erased it. What did not change was the way they were made: neither was modelled from the household side before it was set. And the benefit cap, which bites hardest on the same large families, remains.

<Sidenote number={14}>Universal Credit (Removal of Two Child Limit) Act 2026 (c. 13); Department for Education, free school meals eligibility for Universal Credit households from September 2026.</Sidenote>

### Who counts now

The most comprehensive contemporary expression of the grocery-list tradition is not produced by a statistical agency. Trussell food banks provided more than 2.6 million emergency food parcels in 2025, down 12 per cent on 2024 but far above the levels of the mid-2010s; 62 per cent went to families with children.<sup aria-describedby="sn-15">15</sup> Each referral is made by a GP, social worker, school, or adviser who records the household's composition, its income source, and the reasons it ran out — a case-by-case record of what the benefit system did not provide, and for whom.

<Sidenote number={15}>Trussell, end-of-year statistics for 2025 (published 11 March 2026). Trussell now reports by calendar year; its previous record was more than 3.1 million parcels in 2023–24.</Sidenote>

That record can show what administrative data, by design, cannot. Asked whether benefit sanctions drive people to food banks, the Department for Work and Pensions has answered that there is no evidence of a causal link — an answer available because its systems record that a sanction was applied, not what happened after. Rachel Loopstra and colleagues linked food bank referral data to sanction records across local authorities and found that more sanctions were followed by more people fed by food banks, an association they read as a lower bound because people with no food bank nearby left no trace.<sup aria-describedby="sn-16">16</sup> "No evidence" was not a finding. It was the consequence of deciding what not to collect.

<Sidenote number={16}>Loopstra, Fledderjohann, Reeves, and Stuckler, "Impact of Welfare Benefit Sanctioning on Food Insecurity: A Dynamic Cross-Area Study of Food Bank Usage in the UK," *Journal of Social Policy* (2018).</Sidenote>

The families in those referral records — the parent caught in the taper, the household whose third child generated no child element for nine years, the claimant whose sanction letter came weeks before the food bank voucher — are the families the grocery-list tradition was built to count, from Orshansky's food plans to Tillmon's budgets to Townsend's index. In each case the count was more honest than the administrative alternative. In each case the administrative alternative won.

### Connection Forward

Part II examines what happened when the ideology of optimisation colonised the administrative infrastructure the postwar welfare state's simplifications had left in place. The cybernetic, operations-research, and algorithmic traditions that Chapter 5 begins to examine inherited a welfare mathematics that had already made a specific set of political choices: it had traded accuracy for communicability and the grocery receipt for the index number. Part II asks how those choices were taken further, faster, and with far more ideological confidence.
