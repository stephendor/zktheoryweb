## Synopsis

In the same decade, two welfare states set out to replace the Poor Law's moral inquisition with actuarial calculation. In London, William Beveridge's 1942 Report built a flat-rate contribution and benefit around a male industrial worker whose wife's welfare was derived from his record. In Stockholm, Alva and Gunnar Myrdal's population programme, and later the ATP pension, built an earnings-related system around two earners whose childcare was a public responsibility. Both used the same tools — contribution records, replacement rates, eligibility thresholds, population projections — and both were real advances on what came before. But the choice of standard person was political, and it shaped who could appear in the formula. In Britain, universalism carried conditions that made departures from the standard household calculable: a contribution record that domestic and seasonal work did not generate, a wage stop that capped benefit at previous wages, a cohabitation rule that sent officials into women's wardrobes. The same formula landed differently on industrial Clydeside and in the crofting counties. The statistical infrastructure built to monitor the system showed by 1965 that poverty had not been abolished, and by 1988 means-testing had returned as the system's working core. The Poor Law's question did not disappear. It was encoded.

## 1. Two Tables, Two Arithmetics: Beveridge and Myrdal

In the winter of 1942, two documents were shaping the postwar welfare state, and neither acknowledged the other. In London, William Beveridge's *Social Insurance and Allied Services* was published on 1 December. In Stockholm, Alva Myrdal's *Nation and Family*, published the year before, was circulating among the people building the *Folkhem*, the People's Home. Both proposed to replace the Poor Law's moral inquisition with actuarial calculation at population scale — contribution records, benefit schedules, eligibility thresholds, projected claim rates — administered without reference to the character of any claimant. And both embedded, in apparently technical details, a decision about who the standard person at the centre of the welfare state would be.

They chose differently.

Beveridge's standard person was a male industrial worker. One weekly insurance stamp, the same for a factory hand in Salford and a clerk in Surbiton, bought a standard package of benefits — an ideal of social citizenship as equal membership in a risk pool. The benefit, 40 shillings a week for a man and his wife and 24 for a single adult, was a subsistence floor derived partly from Rowntree's nutritional standards and partly from the Government Actuary's projections of what contributions could sustain.<sup aria-describedby="sn-1">1</sup> This was quantification doing what Theodore Porter identifies as its characteristic work in government: substituting impersonal rules for personal judgment, so that distributional decisions look technical.<sup aria-describedby="sn-2">2</sup> The rates looked like the output of an actuarial model. They were political choices.

<Sidenote number={1}>Beveridge, *Social Insurance and Allied Services* (1942); Rowntree, *Poverty: A Study of Town Life* (1901).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={2}>Porter, *Trust in Numbers* (1995).</Sidenote>

The worker was assumed to be a man; his wife was assumed to be at home, and her welfare was derived from his. The 40-shilling couple rate was not 24 shillings for her and 16 for him; it was 40 shillings for a household defined by his employment. Nancy Fraser names the resulting structure: a "masculine" subsystem of social insurance, tied to employment and conferring rights, beside a "feminine" subsystem of assistance, conferring dependence.<sup aria-describedby="sn-3">3</sup> For those whose lives fitted the model, it worked as advertised. For everyone else, entitlement turned on conditions built into the formula.

<Sidenote number={3}>Fraser, "After the Family Wage: Gender Equity and the Welfare State," *Political Theory* (1994).</Sidenote>

Myrdal started from a different question. Sweden's fertility rate had fallen from over four children per woman to under 1.7 by the early 1930s. *Crisis in the Population Question*, written with Gunnar Myrdal in 1934, treated the fall as a rational response to cramped housing, insecure income, and the impossibility of combining paid work with childcare. *Nation and Family* made a move Beveridge never made: it brought domestic labour and the organisation of the household into public policy as matters of economic calculation.<sup aria-describedby="sn-4">4</sup> Child allowances, public childcare, school meals, and housing with rational kitchens were justified in the same terms Beveridge used for National Insurance — projected costs and population-level sustainability. Myrdal's standard person was a dual-earner household. Where Beveridge's flat rate asked the minimum question — what is the least a person needs to survive? — the Swedish earnings-related model asked the continuity question: what does a person need to keep their living standard across a life?

<Sidenote number={4}>A. and G. Myrdal, *Kris i befolkningsfrågan* (1934); A. Myrdal, *Nation and Family* (1941).</Sidenote>

Sweden did not solve the problem Britain encoded. The dual-earner household was its own fiction: it presupposed a white, heterosexual nuclear family, treated population as an object to be planned in ways that shared more with its eugenic contemporaries than celebratory accounts admit, and coexisted for decades with women's work that stayed part-time, interrupted, and lower-paid. But the contrast matters. Using the same tools, two settlements made different choices about the person the formula described. The mathematics did not force either choice; the technical form made both harder to see.

### Who gets to be an architect

Janet Philip — trained in mathematics at St Andrews, for nearly two decades School Secretary at the London School of Economics — made the Beveridge Report publishable. Ann Oakley estimates her drafting and revision at some 200,000 words, recovered from the Beveridge supplementary papers decades later; her name is nowhere in the published document.<sup aria-describedby="sn-5">5</sup> Alva Myrdal's name is on the cover of *Nation and Family*; she became a cabinet minister and a Nobel Peace Prize laureate. Beveridge's system encoded a world in which women's work, including the intellectual work of making the Report legible, was absorbed into male authorship and male contribution records. Myrdal's, for all its limits, expected women to appear as economic actors with their own records and their own names. The arithmetic did not determine the outcome. It made one far more likely than the other.

<Sidenote number={5}>Oakley, *Forgotten Wives: How Women Get Written Out of History* (2023).</Sidenote>

## 2. From Moral Failure to System Parameter: What the Actuarial Turn Changed

The Poor Law asked one question: do you deserve relief? The 1834 New Poor Law formalised the answer as the workhouse test, and the examination was designed to humiliate. Under it, T. H. Marshall wrote, paupers "ceased to be citizens in any true sense of the word."<sup aria-describedby="sn-6">6</sup> Beveridge's welfare state asked a different question: has this person paid enough contributions? A clerk checked a card instead of a magistrate weighing character. The record was impersonal and consistent, and it conferred a right rather than a concession — the culmination of Marshall's sequence of civil, political, and social rights. It did not ask whether you drank or whether your house was clean. For millions of men in stable industrial work, this was liberation from a century of moral surveillance.

<Sidenote number={6}>Marshall, *Citizenship and Social Class* (1950), p. 33.</Sidenote>

The actuarial model performed its own abstraction. The claimant became a parameter. The Report mapped everyone into a grid — insurance class, age, sex, marital status, dependency, employment — and each cell carried a fixed schedule. A Class I male employee paid 4 shillings and 3 pence a week and, if unemployed, received 40 shillings if married and 24 if single.<sup aria-describedby="sn-7">7</sup> The system was not interested in his rent or his children's needs, only in which cell he occupied. Ian Hacking's "looping effect" works here through governance: after the National Insurance Act 1946 there were contributors and claimants, and to be a contributor was to have a record and a right, while to be a dependant was to have every claim mediated through someone else's employment.<sup aria-describedby="sn-8">8</sup> The categories did not describe that difference. They produced it, by recording one person's contributions and not another's.

<Sidenote number={7}>Beveridge 1942.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={8}>Hacking, "Making Up People" (1986); Fraser 1994.</Sidenote>

James Scott's *legibility* names what the grid accomplished and what it cost.<sup aria-describedby="sn-9">9</sup> National Insurance could be run through the post office precisely because it needed to know nothing about the people it served. The Poor Law relieving officer, for all the system's ugliness, knew the households in his parish and the local price of bread and coal; the National Insurance clerk did not need to. The cost of consistency was the loss of particularity, and because particularity is where actual need lives, the cost fell hardest on people whose needs did not fit. The move from moral assessment to parametric classification was at once a move towards justice and a move towards abstraction, and no formula resolves the tension. What changed was the kind of injustice. The Poor Law's was personal and arbitrary. The postwar state's was structural: the woman whose labour was domestic, the crofter whose income was seasonal, the migrant whose record was interrupted did not fail a moral test. They fell outside a grid.

<Sidenote number={9}>Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998).</Sidenote>

## 3. The Conditions: Where Universalism Hid Its Exclusions

The 1942 Report set out to abolish Want. Its language was universal; its conditions were not. Three of them each treated one way of living as normal and made the cost of departing from it calculable.

### The contribution record

The contribution card was the document of a right. It was also a barrier. Contribution records tracked formal wage labour, and that was not how everyone who sustained a household worked. Married women doing domestic labour — the large majority of married women in 1948 — generated no stamps. Agricultural workers, seasonal labourers, and domestic servants generated thin records that activated thin entitlements.<sup aria-describedby="sn-10">10</sup> The requirement did not ask whether a person's circumstances warranted support, only whether their work had generated paperwork. A married woman's access to benefit depended on her husband's record and could end in circumstances the rules were not built for: separation without divorce, desertion, widowhood before pension age.

<Sidenote number={10}>Marshall 1950; Beveridge 1942; Fraser 1994.</Sidenote>

### The wage stop

The wage stop turned Beveridge's incentive principle — that the national minimum should leave room for people to provide more for themselves — into an arithmetic of punishment. Let $R$ be a household's requirements under the scale rates, what the system itself had determined the family needed, and $W$ the breadwinner's normal net earnings in work. If $R > W$, the National Assistance Board, and later the Supplementary Benefits Commission, paid benefit at $W$, not $R$.<sup aria-describedby="sn-11">11</sup> The family's officially assessed need was refused because meeting it would make unemployment pay more than work. A 1972 Commons debate made the result plain: families with identical needs received different benefits solely because their previous wages differed.<sup aria-describedby="sn-12">12</sup> Those worst treated in work received the lowest benefits out of it, and large families — four children and a rent — were held below the subsistence figure Beveridge's own arithmetic had set.

<Sidenote number={11}>Supplementary Benefits Commission, report on the operation of the wage stop (1967).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={12}>House of Commons, debate on the wage stop (1972).</Sidenote>

This is mechanical objectivity in Porter's sense: a distributive judgment, that people on benefit should not be better off than the lowest-paid in work, dressed as a mathematical constraint. The Child Poverty Action Group campaigned against it, and the wage stop went in 1975.<sup aria-describedby="sn-13">13</sup> Its logic did not. The household benefit cap, introduced in 2013, again sets a ceiling on what a household can receive regardless of assessed need, and again bites hardest on large families with high housing costs. In April 2026 the government abolished the two-child limit on Universal Credit; the cap stayed, and the government's own impact assessment expected around 70,000 families not to receive the full increase because the cap absorbed it.<sup aria-describedby="sn-14">14</sup> Once "what is this family entitled to?" is answered by a parameter ceiling, the answer is trivial to encode in software. The technology of administration changes. The arithmetic of incentive remains.

<Sidenote number={13}>Child Poverty Action Group, *The Wage Stop: Poverty by Official Policy* (1971).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={14}>Reported by Save the Children, "Two-child limit fact sheet" (2026), and Contact, "Contact welcomes abolition of the two-child limit" (2026), citing the DWP impact assessment.</Sidenote>

### The cohabitation rule

The Poor Law's most invasive tool was the household inspection. The postwar welfare state appeared to abolish it; it survived as the household aggregation rule. National Assistance made the married couple the unit of assessment, so a wife's needs were assessed against her husband's resources. Through the 1950s and 1960s the rule was extended to unmarried women judged to be "living together as husband and wife": a woman so judged had her benefit assessed against the man's income whether or not he supported her, though he had no legal duty to do so.<sup aria-describedby="sn-15">15</sup> Because the rule turned on domestic arrangements, officials made unannounced visits, examined bedrooms and wardrobes for signs of a man, and questioned neighbours. The question had changed from "do you deserve relief?" to "are you living with a man?" The intrusion was the same. In Fraser's terms this was an interpretation of need — that a woman living with a man had her needs met by him — presented as a rule about who counted as husband or wife. The judgment was hidden inside the formula.

<Sidenote number={15}>Supplementary Benefits Commission, *Cohabitation: The Administration of the Relevant Provisions of the Ministry of Social Security Act 1966* (1971).</Sidenote>

## 4. The Folkhem's Mathematics: From Myrdal's Vision to ATP's Formula

For the Myrdals, fertility was a policy variable: if the state wanted children, it had to change the conditions that made paid work and motherhood incompatible.<sup aria-describedby="sn-16">16</sup> Beveridge recognised women's domestic labour by folding them into their husbands' insurance class, which erased their economic identity. Myrdal recognised it by proposing to rebuild the institutions that made it incompatible with paid work, which required women to be economic actors in their own right. Ernst Wigforss, Social Democratic finance minister for most of 1932–49, treated spending on housing, child health, and education as investment, and the *Folkhem* accumulated piece by piece: school meals from 1946, universal child allowances from 1948, public childcare from the 1960s.

<Sidenote number={16}>Myrdal and Myrdal 1934; Myrdal 1941.</Sidenote>

### ATP: the formula

The centrepiece was the ATP earnings-related pension, introduced in 1960 after a political battle that dominated Swedish politics through the late 1950s. Before it, white-collar workers had occupational pensions and most blue-collar workers did not; the Social Democrats and the LO unions argued that only a statutory scheme could prevent a two-tier old age. ATP's structure encodes a different logic from Beveridge's at every level. A full pension required thirty years of covered work; the pension base was the average of the fifteen best earnings years; a full pension replaced 60 per cent of that base, on top of the flat-rate *folkpension*. Writing $E_{15}$ for the fifteen-best-years average:

$$
\text{ATP} = 0.6 \times E_{15} \times \frac{\text{years worked}}{30}
$$

Employers financed it through payroll contributions, and individual earnings were tracked from the start.<sup aria-describedby="sn-17">17</sup> Two features matter. ATP tracked individual earnings, not household composition: there was no dependent-wife category and no derived benefit. And it smoothed income across a life rather than setting a floor: 60 per cent was a middle-class aspiration, chosen to bind white-collar workers into a public system. Richard Titmuss's distinction between institutional and residual welfare clarifies the difference: both systems were institutional, but Beveridge included people through citizenship, ATP through interest.<sup aria-describedby="sn-18">18</sup>

<Sidenote number={17}>Palme, "Features of the Swedish Pension Reform" (2005).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={18}>Titmuss, *Essays on "The Welfare State"* (1958).</Sidenote>

### A formally neutral formula

ATP was formally gender-neutral. It was not substantively neutral, because the labour market it recorded was not. Swedish women's work in the 1960s and 1970s was concentrated in part-time, lower-paid, more interrupted jobs. A woman who worked part-time for fifteen years to manage childcare accrued points for her actual hours and wages, and the fifteen-best-years rule favoured careers that peaked sharply in mid-life — disproportionately men's. Swedish women ended up with lower pensions not because the formula distinguished between them but because it treated an unequal labour market as if its inequalities were irrelevant.<sup aria-describedby="sn-19">19</sup> So Sweden shows that the dependent-wife category was a British design choice, not a mathematical necessity — and that configuring the system differently is not the same as escaping the problem. ATP's standard person, a worker with a continuous thirty-year career, was also a fiction, one that disadvantaged those who fell short of full-time work rather than those outside formal work altogether. The arithmetic of welfare is political before it is technical.

<Sidenote number={19}>Palme 2005.</Sidenote>

## 5. The Statistical Infrastructure: Building the Machinery of Governance

Beveridge's welfare state needed statistical answers. Could contributions sustain projected claims? Were flat-rate benefits still adequate once prices rose? Was poverty falling? In 1942 the infrastructure to answer did not exist. Some was under construction: in January 1941 Churchill had instructed the Cabinet Secretary to set up a central statistical office so that departments worked from agreed figures, with Harry Campion as its first Director.<sup aria-describedby="sn-20">20</sup> Its first major achievement was the national income and expenditure accounts. GDP is not a discovery but a set of conventions — market transactions count as production, unpaid domestic labour does not — and those conventions have framed every later argument about what "the national income can afford."

<Sidenote number={20}>War Cabinet, *Establishment of the Central Statistical Office* (1941).</Sidenote>

### The survey that refuted its owner

The Family Expenditure Survey, continuous from 1957, began with a mundane need: weights for the Retail Price Index. Some 7,000 to 10,000 households a year kept two-week diaries of every purchase.<sup aria-describedby="sn-21">21</sup> It was built for price statistics, not poverty measurement. What it produced, incidentally, was the first systematic picture of household consumption across incomes, household types, and regions. Brian Abel-Smith and Peter Townsend saw what was in the data before the administrators did. Setting a poverty line at 1.4 times National Assistance scales, *The Poor and the Poorest* (1965) found that poverty had not been abolished; it had persisted and, by their measure, grown.<sup aria-describedby="sn-22">22</sup> The welfare state had generated the data that refuted its own claims. The data's political uses followed from what it could do, not what it was built to do. The General Household Survey, from 1971, extended the reach to employment, pensions, health, family composition, income, and housing.<sup aria-describedby="sn-23">23</sup>

<Sidenote number={21}>Ministry of Labour, *Family Expenditure Survey 1957: First Report*.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={22}>Abel-Smith and Townsend, *The Poor and the Poorest* (1965).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={23}>Office of Population Censuses and Surveys, *The General Household Survey: Introductory Report* (1973).</Sidenote>

In Scott's terms, the accounts, the surveys, and the contribution records built a grid through which the state could see its population — not the person with her particular rent, but the income decile and the family type. The grid made redistribution administrable. It also outlasted the settlement that produced it. The household the survey measured became the household benefit rules would regulate; the employment status it tracked became the status conditionality would police; the income band became what means-testing would interrogate. None of this was built to create an automated poorhouse. But the machinery of governance is politically persistent. The categories created to make redistribution possible survived the redistribution and went to work for something else.

## 6. The Scottish Settlement: Differential Application and Its Limits

Scotland did not have one relationship to Beveridge's model. It had two. In the industrial belt — Glasgow, Motherwell, Dundee, the mining villages of Fife and Lanarkshire, the Clyde shipyards — the man working full-time in one heavy industry, stamped weekly by one employer, came closer to the system's standard person than almost anywhere in Britain. In the crofting counties the standard person did not exist.

Scotland had also run its own poor relief system for a century. The Poor Law (Scotland) Act 1845 created the Board of Supervision in Edinburgh to oversee parochial boards that did not insist on the workhouse test, relied heavily on outdoor relief, and gave the able-bodied unemployed no legal right to relief until the 1920s.<sup aria-describedby="sn-24">24</sup> The Board's records show a system that governed through local judgment and case-by-case negotiation over poorhouses, disputed settlement, and complaints from refused applicants.<sup aria-describedby="sn-25">25</sup> The National Assistance Act 1948 abolished it, but the county and burgh councils that had run public assistance became the local nodes of the new system, and officials carried the old assumptions into it. The new system was layered on the old rather than replacing it cleanly.

<Sidenote number={24}>Poor Law Amendment (Scotland) Act 1845.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={25}>Records of the Board of Supervision for the Relief of the Poor in Scotland, National Records of Scotland, HH23.</Sidenote>

Industrial Clydeside absorbed the model most readily, and that was its vulnerability. When the heavy industries contracted from the 1960s and collapsed in the 1980s, the communities whose welfare had been most thoroughly organised around the breadwinner's contribution record lost not only their incomes but their structural eligibility for the contributory benefits they had been promised. The system's universalism had always been conditional on the industries continuing to employ the men whose records it counted on.

In the crofting counties the mismatch was structural from the start. The Crofters Holdings (Scotland) Act 1886 created a distinct tenure across seven counties.<sup aria-describedby="sn-26">26</sup> Crofts were small holdings, never sufficient alone; households combined cultivation and livestock with seasonal labour, fishing, and later local wage work. A crofter who also worked seasonally in construction was an employee for part of the year and self-employed for the rest. The self-employed paid flat-rate contributions that carried a retirement pension but no unemployment benefit — the contingency seasonal work made most relevant — and unemployment benefit was not payable for days on which a worker "does not in the normal course expect to be working."<sup aria-describedby="sn-27">27</sup> The formula did not distinguish a Glasgow shipyard worker temporarily laid off from a Harris crofter at the end of the fishing season. It noted that one had a full record and a normal working pattern, and the other did not.

<Sidenote number={26}>Crofters Holdings (Scotland) Act 1886.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={27}>House of Commons, debate on National Insurance for seasonal and agricultural workers (1960).</Sidenote>

The crofting counties were statistically thin, too: the Family Expenditure Survey's national sample left them represented by a handful of cases in any year, so high transport costs, non-cash production, and distance from services barely registered in national poverty lines.<sup aria-describedby="sn-28">28</sup> In a 1968 House of Lords debate, speakers described chronic underemployment and steady out-migration of the young.<sup aria-describedby="sn-29">29</sup> The welfare state reached these communities mainly through means-tested National Assistance — the residual tier Beveridge designed as a last resort became the main support for the people his universal system could not reach.

<Sidenote number={28}>Abel-Smith and Townsend 1965.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={29}>House of Lords, debate on the Highlands and Islands (1968).</Sidenote>

## 7. Cracks in the Foundation: The Return of Means-Testing

National Assistance was meant to be residual in the strict sense: a temporary provision for the few whose needs insurance and full employment could not meet. Beveridge opposed means tests and designed National Insurance to make them unnecessary.<sup aria-describedby="sn-30">30</sup> It did not stay residual. The Ministry of Social Security Act 1966 replaced National Assistance with Supplementary Benefit: statutory entitlement replaced discretion, scale rates rose roughly 27 per cent above 1964 levels against an 8.8 per cent rise in prices, and the government for the first time advertised for claimants.<sup aria-describedby="sn-31">31</sup> The safety net was improved rather than the contributory model questioned.

<Sidenote number={30}>Institute for Fiscal Studies, *Means-Testing and the Legacy of the Beveridge Settlement* (2023).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={31}>Ministry of Social Security Act 1966 and related parliamentary proceedings.</Sidenote>

The Family Income Supplement, introduced at the start of the 1970s, went further: it topped up the wages of low-paid full-time workers with children, paying half the gap between their earnings and a prescribed threshold.<sup aria-describedby="sn-32">32</sup> Beveridge assumed full-time, insured work would keep a household above the floor. The supplement conceded that the labour market itself was producing the need the welfare state was meant to prevent. The vocabulary shifted before the architecture did. In the New Right's reframing of the 1970s and 1980s, the question was no longer whether a person had contributed but whether they were making enough effort to work. "Deserving" and "undeserving" returned as "incentives" and "targeting."

<Sidenote number={32}>Family Income Supplements Act.</Sidenote>

### 1986: the architecture of the trap

The Social Security Act 1986, implemented in April 1988, built that reframing into the system. Income Support replaced Supplementary Benefit; anyone working 24 hours a week or more was excluded and directed to Family Credit if they had children, or to nothing if they did not. Family Credit was withdrawn at 70 per cent of net income above a threshold.<sup aria-describedby="sn-33">33</sup> Combined with income tax and National Insurance, effective withdrawal rates could exceed 90 per cent: a household earning £10 more could keep less than £1 of it. The poverty trap was not a malfunction. It was the system's topology. With Housing Benefit alongside, this income-related architecture set the shape of UK means-tested support until Universal Credit.<sup aria-describedby="sn-34">34</sup> The same Act cut back the State Earnings-Related Pension Scheme, Britain's limited answer to ATP, and paid employees to contract out into personal pensions — a reorganisation of risk, with more of it borne privately and more of the residue means-tested at the bottom.

<Sidenote number={33}>Social Security Act 1986.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={34}>Institute for Fiscal Studies 2023.</Sidenote>

### Encoded, not abolished

By 1988 the contribution record had not insulated the population from means-testing. The wage stop was gone, but the taper had taken up its logic; the cohabitation rule survived in Income Support's household aggregation. Income Support, Family Credit, and Housing Benefit were rule-based calculations — national scale rates, standard disregards, detailed rules on income and capital — that suited computers precisely because they had turned "does this person deserve relief?" into "does this household's income fall below this threshold, given these parameters?" Beveridge designed his system to make the Poor Law's question redundant. The question returned in a new grammar of household resources, scale rates, hours rules, and tapers. The moral assessment had not been abolished. It had been encoded.

### Connection Forward

Chapter 4 asks what was simultaneously present and defeated in the same decades: a materially specific, household-grounded counter-mathematics to the actuarial model, built from grocery receipts, welfare rights campaigns, and deprivation surveys. Understanding why that alternative lost requires understanding what the postwar welfare state had already built and why its administrative logic so consistently resolved toward simplification.
