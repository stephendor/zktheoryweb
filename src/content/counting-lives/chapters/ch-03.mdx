## Synopsis

In the same decade, two welfare states set out to replace the Poor Law's moral inquisition with actuarial calculation. In London, William Beveridge's 1942 Report built a flat-rate contribution and benefit around a male industrial worker whose wife's welfare was derived from his record. In Stockholm, Alva and Gunnar Myrdal's population programme, and later the ATP pension, built an earnings-related system around two earners whose childcare was a public responsibility. Both used the same tools — contribution records, replacement rates, eligibility thresholds, population projections — and both were real advances on what came before. But the choice of standard person was political, and it shaped who could appear in the formula. In Britain, universalism carried conditions that made departures from the standard household calculable: a contribution record that domestic and seasonal work did not generate, a wage stop that capped benefit at previous wages, a cohabitation rule that sent officials into women's wardrobes. The same formula landed differently on industrial Clydeside and in the crofting counties. The statistical infrastructure built to monitor the system — national accounts, the Family Expenditure Survey, the General Household Survey — showed by 1965 that poverty had not been abolished, and by 1988 means-testing had returned as the system's working core. The Poor Law's question did not disappear. It was encoded.

## 1. Two Tables, Two Arithmetics: Beveridge and Myrdal

In the winter of 1942, two documents were shaping the postwar welfare state, and neither acknowledged the other. In London, William Beveridge's *Social Insurance and Allied Services* was published on 1 December to queues outside His Majesty's Stationery Office — some 635,000 copies of a government report sold. In Stockholm, Alva Myrdal's *Nation and Family: The Swedish Experiment in Democratic Family and Population Policy*, published the year before, was circulating among the people building what would become the *Folkhem*, the People's Home. Both proposed to replace the Poor Law's moral inquisition with actuarial calculation at population scale: contribution records, benefit schedules, eligibility thresholds, and projected claim rates that could be administered and defended without reference to the character of any individual claimant. And both embedded, in apparently technical details, a political decision about who the standard person at the centre of the welfare state would be.

They chose differently.

Beveridge's standard person was a male industrial worker. The flat-rate model — one weekly insurance stamp, the same for every contributor regardless of income, buying a standard package of benefits — expressed a democratic ideal of social citizenship as equal membership in a risk pool. The contribution was the same for a factory hand in Salford and a clerk in Surbiton because the principle was equality of status, not of income. The benefit — 40 shillings a week for a man and his wife, 24 for a single adult — was designed as a subsistence floor, not a replacement for earnings. Beveridge derived the figures partly from Rowntree's nutritional standards, uplifted by an assumed 25 per cent rise in the postwar cost of living, and partly from the Government Actuary's projections of what contribution rates could sustain over twenty years. The Social Security Budget in Part IV of the Report is the system's actuarial backbone: £697 million projected for 1945 and £858 million for 1965, each figure broken down by benefit and source of finance, each resting on assumed sickness rates, unemployment levels, and demographic trajectories.<sup aria-describedby="sn-1">1</sup>

<Sidenote number={1}>Beveridge, *Social Insurance and Allied Services* (1942); Rowntree, *Poverty: A Study of Town Life* (1901).</Sidenote>

This was quantification doing what Theodore Porter identifies as its characteristic work in government: substituting impersonal rules for personal judgment, so that distributional decisions look technical rather than political.<sup aria-describedby="sn-2">2</sup> Beveridge's rates looked like the output of an actuarial model. They were political choices. The flat rate's egalitarianism was real, and a genuine advance on the parish's moral assessment. It was also incomplete in ways that were structural, not accidental.

<Sidenote number={2}>Porter, *Trust in Numbers* (1995).</Sidenote>

The person insured was a worker; the worker was assumed to be a man; his wife was assumed to be at home, and her welfare was derived from his. She appeared in the formula not as a contributor with her own record but as a dependant. The 40-shilling couple rate was not 24 shillings for her and 16 for him; it was 40 shillings for a household defined by his employment. Nancy Fraser names the resulting structure: a "masculine" subsystem of social insurance, tied to labour-market participation and conferring rights, alongside a "feminine" subsystem of assistance for those outside formal employment, conferring dependence.<sup aria-describedby="sn-3">3</sup> Beveridge's universalism was built on the first track. For those whose lives fitted the model — employed, male, paying stamps — it worked as advertised. For everyone else, entitlement turned on conditions built into the formula.

<Sidenote number={3}>Fraser, "After the Family Wage: Gender Equity and the Welfare State," *Political Theory* (1994).</Sidenote>

Myrdal started from a different question. Where Beveridge asked how to insure a breadwinner against the Five Giants — Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, Idleness — Myrdal asked how to build a society in which women's paid employment and motherhood could coexist. Sweden's fertility rate had fallen from over four children per woman at the turn of the century to under 1.7 by the early 1930s. *Crisis in the Population Question*, which she wrote with Gunnar Myrdal in 1934, reframed the fall not as moral decline but as a rational response to inadequate housing, insecure income, and the impossibility of combining paid work with childcare. *Nation and Family* carried the programme to an international audience, and its core was a move Beveridge never made: bringing domestic labour, childrearing, and the organisation of the household into public policy as matters of economic calculation rather than private arrangement.<sup aria-describedby="sn-4">4</sup> Universal child allowances, public childcare, school meals, maternal and child health clinics, and housing with rational kitchens were justified in the same terms Beveridge used for National Insurance: projected costs, anticipated returns, population-level sustainability. The mathematics was the same kind. The person at its centre was not.

<Sidenote number={4}>A. and G. Myrdal, *Kris i befolkningsfrågan* (1934); A. Myrdal, *Nation and Family* (1941).</Sidenote>

Myrdal's standard person was a dual-earner household. If both parents were economic actors, both needed individual contribution records, and benefits tied to earnings rather than flat-rate citizenship could offer the security that would bind the middle class into a universal system. Beveridge's flat rate asked the minimum question: what is the least a person needs to survive? The Swedish earnings-related model asked the continuity question: what does a person need to maintain their living standard across a life? The difference in the question produced a difference in the formula, and the difference in the formula produced a difference in who was visible inside it.

Sweden did not solve the problem Britain encoded. Myrdal's dual-earner household was its own fiction: it presupposed a white, heterosexual nuclear family; it treated population as an object to be planned, sharing more with its eugenic contemporaries than celebratory accounts like to admit; and Swedish women's employment remained part-time, interrupted, and lower-paid than men's for decades after the policy framework was in place. The mathematics of the dual-earner welfare state still had a standard person at its centre. She was simply a different fiction from Beveridge's dependent wife. The contrast matters because both settlements used the same tools — contribution records, replacement ratios, eligibility thresholds, population projections — and made different choices about the person the formula described. The mathematics did not force either choice. The choices were political decisions embedded in technical parameters, and the technical form made them harder to see.

### Who gets to be an architect

Janet Philip — trained in mathematics at St Andrews, for nearly two decades School Secretary at the London School of Economics — made the Beveridge Report publishable. Ann Oakley estimates that her drafting and revision ran to some 200,000 words. She married Beveridge two weeks after the Report appeared. Her name is nowhere in the published document, the headlines, or the standard histories; Oakley recovered the evidence from the Beveridge supplementary papers, which Philip's son donated in 1974.<sup aria-describedby="sn-5">5</sup> Alva Myrdal's name, by contrast, is on the cover of *Nation and Family*. She became a cabinet minister, a UNESCO director, and a Nobel Peace Prize laureate. The difference is not a biographical accident. Beveridge's system encoded a world in which women's work — including the intellectual work of making the Report legible — was absorbed into male authorship and male contribution records. Myrdal's system, for all its limits, expected women to appear as economic actors with their own records and their own names. The arithmetic did not determine the outcome. It made one far more likely than the other.

<Sidenote number={5}>Oakley, *Forgotten Wives: How Women Get Written Out of History* (2023).</Sidenote>

## 2. From Moral Failure to System Parameter: What the Actuarial Turn Changed

The Poor Law asked one question of the person standing before it: do you deserve relief? The answer depended on the magistrate's assessment of character, the relieving officer's impression of the household, the parish's judgment about whether poverty was misfortune or moral failure. The 1834 New Poor Law formalised the assessment as the workhouse test — destitution proved by accepting conditions deliberately made worse than those of the lowest independent labourer — and the examination was designed to humiliate. Under it, T. H. Marshall wrote, paupers "suffered a species of attainder" and "ceased to be citizens in any true sense of the word."<sup aria-describedby="sn-6">6</sup>

<Sidenote number={6}>Marshall, *Citizenship and Social Class* (1950), p. 33.</Sidenote>

Beveridge's welfare state asked a different question: has this person paid enough contributions to qualify? A clerk checked a contribution card instead of a magistrate weighing character. The record was impersonal and consistent, and it conferred a right rather than a concession — the point on which Marshall's account of social citizenship turns, completing his sequence of civil, political, and social rights with a guaranteed minimum of economic security. The same record produced the same benefit in Salford and Surbiton, Dundee and Dover. It did not ask whether you drank, whether your house was clean, whether your wife was industrious. It asked whether you had paid your stamps. For millions of men in stable industrial work — the system's intended subjects — this was liberation from a century of moral surveillance.

The actuarial model performed its own abstraction. The claimant became a parameter. The Report mapped everyone in the covered population into a finite grid — insurance class, age band, sex, marital status, dependency, employment status — and each cell carried a fixed schedule. A Class I male employee aged 21 or over paid 4 shillings and 3 pence a week, his employer 3 shillings and 3 pence; if unemployed or disabled he received 40 shillings a week if married, 24 if single.<sup aria-describedby="sn-7">7</sup> The system was not interested in whether his rent was high, whether his children had particular needs, whether his wife's health needed more than the standard rate covered. It was interested in which cell of the table he occupied. The table was the system.

<Sidenote number={7}>Beveridge 1942.</Sidenote>

Ian Hacking's "looping effect" — the feedback between classification and the people classified — works here through governance rather than identity.<sup aria-describedby="sn-8">8</sup> Before the National Insurance Act 1946 there were workers, the unemployed, the sick, the old. After it there were contributors and claimants, categories carrying legal entitlements, procedures, and relationships to the state that the older vocabulary had not contained. Nobody organised a movement around being a Class I contributor. But to be a contributor was to have a record, to accumulate entitlement, to stand in a rights-bearing relation to the state; to be a dependant was to lack a record of one's own, with every claim mediated through another person's employment. The categories did not describe a pre-existing difference between those positions. They produced it, by recording one person's contributions and not another's. Fraser's two subsystems were manufactured by the same classificatory machinery.

<Sidenote number={8}>Hacking, "Making Up People" (1986); Fraser 1994.</Sidenote>

James Scott's *legibility* names what the grid accomplished and what it cost.<sup aria-describedby="sn-9">9</sup> Poverty, illness, unemployment, and old age were lived in particular ways — this woman's rent, that family's medical costs, this crofter's seasonal income, that widow's lack of any income at all — and the system reorganised them into insurance classes, contribution records, and benefit schedules. National Insurance could be run through the post office precisely because it needed to know nothing about the particular circumstances of the people it served. A stamp was a stamp. Scott contrasts the state's schematic knowledge with *mētis*, the situated knowledge of the fisherman who reads the water. Beveridge's model did not crush *mētis* as Soviet collectivisation did; it set it aside, through the ordinary preference for categories that can be administered over knowledge that cannot. The Poor Law relieving officer, for all the system's moral ugliness, knew the households in his parish and the local price of bread and coal. The National Insurance clerk knew none of this and did not need to. The cost of consistency was the loss of particularity, and because particularity is where actual need lives, that cost fell hardest on people whose needs did not fit the categories.

<Sidenote number={9}>Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998).</Sidenote>

The move from moral assessment to parametric classification was at once a move towards justice — consistency, predictability, entitlement as of right — and a move towards abstraction. Both were real, and no formula resolves the tension between them. What changed was the kind of injustice the welfare state produced. The Poor Law's was personal and arbitrary: it depended on who judged you and whether the parish liked the look of you. The postwar state's was structural: it depended on whether your life fitted the categories, and if it did not, the system was not built to notice. The woman whose labour was domestic, the crofter whose income was seasonal, the migrant whose record was interrupted did not fail a moral test. They fell outside a grid. Evidence changed with it — from the relieving officer's testimony to the contribution card, the employment record, the claim form. The stamp record that tracked a worker's contributions from 1948 onward was, administratively, a longitudinal data file; by the early 1970s, contribution records, household surveys, national accounts, and benefit databases had made the population available to government in forms the parish officials of the 1930s could not have imagined.

## 3. The Conditions: Where Universalism Hid Its Exclusions

The 1942 Report set out to abolish Want. Its language was universal; its administrative conditions were not. The contribution record, the wage stop, and the cohabitation rule each treated one way of living as normal and made the cost of departing from it calculable rather than discretionary.

### The contribution record: citizenship through the stamp

The contribution card was the document of a right in Marshall's sense — entitlement as a contributor to the national pool, not charity in administrative dress. It was also a barrier. Class I, adult employees in regular work, generated the stamps; the self-employed contributed at different rates; others contributed where they could. But contribution records tracked formal wage labour, and formal wage labour was not how everyone who sustained households and communities worked. Married women doing domestic labour — the large majority of married women in 1948 — generated no stamps. Agricultural workers, seasonal labourers, and domestic servants, whose work was irregular, poorly paid, and often off the books, generated thin records that activated thin entitlements. Crofting and Highland communities, whose poverty was land-based rather than industrial, fitted especially badly: seasonal, subsistence-oriented, largely unwaged work produced few contributions and so little claim on the national pool.<sup aria-describedby="sn-10">10</sup>

<Sidenote number={10}>Marshall 1950; Beveridge 1942.</Sidenote>

The requirement did not ask whether a person's circumstances warranted support. It asked whether they had been, in a specific legal sense, an employed person in the covered classes. People caring for children or elderly parents, or working land that produced food but not wages, were dependants — or nothing. The category recorded not their economic contribution but whether their work had generated paperwork. A married woman's access to benefit depended on her husband's employment history and could end in circumstances the rules were not built for: separation without divorce, desertion, widowhood before pension age. Fraser describes the result as a nominally universal scheme divided internally along gender lines, with rights-bearing contributors on one side and, on the other, claimants whose need had to be demonstrated rather than established by record.<sup aria-describedby="sn-11">11</sup> The contribution record was the hinge.

<Sidenote number={11}>Fraser 1994.</Sidenote>

### The wage stop: importing the labour market

The wage stop is where Beveridge's incentive principle — that the national minimum should leave room for people to provide more for themselves — became an arithmetic of punishment. If a household's assessed requirements exceeded the breadwinner's previous net earnings, the National Assistance Board, and later the Supplementary Benefits Commission, could stop benefit at those earnings rather than at assessed need.<sup aria-describedby="sn-12">12</sup>

<Sidenote number={12}>Beveridge 1942; Supplementary Benefits Commission, report on the operation of the wage stop (1967).</Sidenote>

The formula is precise. Let $R$ be the household's requirements under the scale rates — what the system itself had determined the family needed — and $W$ the breadwinner's normal net earnings in work. If $R > W$, benefit was paid at $W$, not $R$. The household's officially assessed need was refused because meeting it would make unemployment pay more than work. A 1972 Commons debate made the distributional result plain: families with identical needs received different benefits solely because their previous wages differed.<sup aria-describedby="sn-13">13</sup> The rule imported labour-market inequality straight into the welfare state. Those worst treated in work — low-paid, casual, seasonal workers — received the lowest benefits out of it. Large families were most exposed, because four children and a rent produced a requirement that outran anything a lifetime of casual dock labour had paid. They were held below the national minimum — below the figure Beveridge's own arithmetic called subsistence — because benefit was not allowed to exceed wages, even poverty wages.

<Sidenote number={13}>House of Commons, debate on the wage stop (1972).</Sidenote>

This is mechanical objectivity in Porter's sense: a distributive judgment — that people on benefit should not be better off than the lowest-paid in work — dressed as a mathematical constraint.<sup aria-describedby="sn-14">14</sup> Expressed as a formula, the choice to tie the welfare floor to the labour market did not look like a choice. The Child Poverty Action Group made abolition one of its central demands, and the wage stop went in 1975.<sup aria-describedby="sn-15">15</sup> Its logic did not. The household benefit cap, introduced by the coalition government in 2013, again sets an external ceiling on what a household can receive regardless of assessed need, and again bites hardest on large families with high housing costs in low-wage labour markets. Once "what is this family entitled to?" is answered by a parameter ceiling — previous earnings, then a fixed cap — the answer is trivial to encode in software. The technology of administration changes. The arithmetic of incentive remains. In April 2026 the government abolished the two-child limit on Universal Credit; the benefit cap stayed, and the government's own impact assessment expected around 70,000 families not to receive the full increase because the cap absorbed it.<sup aria-describedby="sn-16">16</sup>

<Sidenote number={14}>Porter 1995, p. 8.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={15}>Child Poverty Action Group, *The Wage Stop: Poverty by Official Policy* (1971).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={16}>Reported by Save the Children, "Two-child limit fact sheet" (2026), and Contact, "Contact welcomes abolition of the two-child limit" (2026), citing the DWP impact assessment. Outside London the cap is £22,020 a year for couples and lone parents.</Sidenote>

### The cohabitation rule: the return of moral surveillance

The Poor Law's most invasive tool was the household inspection: the relieving officer arriving unannounced, assessing the kitchen, questioning neighbours. The postwar welfare state appeared to abolish it. It survived as the household aggregation rule. Beveridge treated husband and wife as a team, contributions made for both and benefits paid to the household; National Assistance made the married couple the unit of assessment, so a wife's needs were assessed against her husband's resources, and if he was in full-time work she had no independent claim.<sup aria-describedby="sn-17">17</sup> Through the 1950s and 1960s, the National Assistance Board and then the Supplementary Benefits Commission extended the rule to unmarried women judged to be "living together as husband and wife." A woman so judged had her benefit assessed against the man's income whether or not he supported her. She could not claim independently; he had no legal duty to support her; the state withdrew support on the assumption that he did.<sup aria-describedby="sn-18">18</sup>

<Sidenote number={17}>Beveridge 1942.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={18}>Supplementary Benefits Commission, *Cohabitation: The Administration of the Relevant Provisions of the Ministry of Social Security Act 1966* (1971).</Sidenote>

What followed was the surveillance the actuarial turn had seemed to end. Because the rule turned on domestic arrangements, officials made unannounced home visits, examined bedrooms and wardrobes for signs of a man, questioned neighbours, and scrutinised women's private lives. The question had changed from "do you deserve relief?" to "are you living with a man?" The intrusion was the same, and so was the effect: the withdrawal of independent income from women whose relationships made the state's model of the family applicable to them. In Fraser's terms this was an institutionalised interpretation of need — that a woman living with a man had her needs met by him, that the household's boundary was the boundary of financial responsibility, that the state supplemented male provision rather than giving women income of their own — presented as a rule about who counted as husband or wife. The judgment was there. It was hidden inside the formula. Beveridge's universalism was universal for male breadwinners in stable industrial work. For lives that departed from that model, the system imposed conditions that decided which households were normal, which labour counted as work, and whose need could register at all.

## 4. The Folkhem's Mathematics: From Myrdal's Vision to ATP's Formula

The Myrdals' 1934 diagnosis treated falling fertility as a rational response to economic insecurity, cramped housing, and the incompatibility of women's paid work with motherhood. If the state wanted children, it had to change those conditions. Fertility was a policy variable.<sup aria-describedby="sn-19">19</sup> That framing had an implication Beveridge's did not. Beveridge was preventing destitution among workers who lost their wages. The Myrdals were designing a society in which modern women's lives, including paid work and public roles, were compatible with having children. Beveridge recognised women's domestic labour by folding them into their husbands' insurance class, which erased their economic identity. Myrdal recognised it by proposing to rebuild the institutions that made it incompatible with paid work, which required women to be economic actors in their own right. *Nation and Family* was blunt about the details: Swedish apartments were too small, their kitchens designed for drudgery; women who worked could not also mother, not from biological incapacity but because the institutions for combining the two did not exist.

<Sidenote number={19}>Myrdal and Myrdal 1934; Myrdal 1941.</Sidenote>

Ernst Wigforss, Social Democratic finance minister almost continuously from 1932 to 1949, turned the vision into fiscal architecture, treating spending on housing, child health, and education as investment that reduced future costs and raised productive capacity. The *Folkhem* was not one law but an accretion: housing construction, school meals from 1946, universal child allowances (*barnbidrag*) from 1947, public childcare expanding from the 1960s.

### ATP: one vote, one formula

The centrepiece was the ATP pension, introduced in 1960 after a battle that dominated Swedish politics through the late 1950s. Before ATP, white-collar workers had occupational pensions negotiated with employers; blue-collar workers organised in the LO unions largely did not, and faced a sharp fall in living standards at retirement. The Social Democrats and LO argued that only a statutory earnings-related scheme could prevent a two-tier old age; employers and the bourgeois parties wanted a voluntary occupational solution. A consultative referendum in 1957 produced no majority. The Social Democratic minority government pressed on, and in March 1959 the bill passed the Riksdag's Second Chamber by a single vote, when the Liberal MP Ture Königson abstained rather than vote against it.<sup aria-describedby="sn-20">20</sup> The dual-earner welfare state's defining institution reached the statute book by the narrowest possible margin.

<Sidenote number={20}>On the *ATP-frågan* and the 1959 vote: "ATP-frågan" and "Pensionsstriden" (source compilations).</Sidenote>

ATP's structure encodes a different actuarial logic from Beveridge's at every level. A full pension required thirty years of covered work; the pension base was the average of the fifteen best earnings years; a full pension replaced 60 per cent of that base, on top of the flat-rate *folkpension*. Writing $E_{15}$ for the fifteen-best-years average:

$$\text{ATP} = 0.6 \times E_{15} \times \frac{\text{years worked}}{30}$$

Employers financed it through payroll contributions — about 3 per cent of payroll in 1960, rising to 10 per cent by 1970 — partly channelled through the public *AP-fonderna*, which became significant actors in Swedish capital markets. Individual earnings were tracked from the start, creating a longitudinal record of labour-market participation for the whole covered population.<sup aria-describedby="sn-21">21</sup>

<Sidenote number={21}>Palme, "Features of the Swedish Pension Reform" (2005).</Sidenote>

Two features matter. ATP tracked individual earnings, not household composition: there was no dependent-wife category, no derived benefit, no contribution made on behalf of a team, and a woman's pension reflected her own employment. And ATP smoothed income across a life rather than setting a floor. Beveridge asked how far a contributor should be allowed to fall; ATP asked what fraction of accustomed earnings should be kept into retirement. Sixty per cent was a middle-class aspiration, not a poverty line, chosen to bind white-collar workers into a public system by matching what their occupational schemes had offered. Richard Titmuss's distinction between institutional and residual welfare clarifies the difference: both systems were institutional, offering entitlement as of right rather than in response to failure, but Beveridge included people through citizenship and ATP through interest — a system good enough for the LO factory worker and the Social Democratic civil servant alike.<sup aria-describedby="sn-22">22</sup>

<Sidenote number={22}>Titmuss, *Essays on "The Welfare State"* (1958).</Sidenote>

### A formally neutral formula

Neither system could account for lives that did not fit its standard person, and those lives were overwhelmingly women's. ATP was formally gender-neutral: it spoke only of individual earnings and years of covered work. It was not substantively neutral, because the labour market it recorded was not. Swedish women's employment in the 1960s and 1970s was concentrated in part-time, lower-paid, more interrupted work — the product of a domestic division of labour that the *Folkhem*'s childcare expansion was reducing but had not dissolved. A woman who worked part-time for fifteen years to manage childcare accrued points for her actual hours and wages. The fifteen-best-years rule loosened the link between lifetime earnings and the pension base, favouring careers that peaked sharply in mid-life — disproportionately men's — over flatter or interrupted ones. Swedish women ended up with lower ATP pensions than men not because the formula distinguished between them but because it treated an unequal labour market as if its inequalities were irrelevant.<sup aria-describedby="sn-23">23</sup>

<Sidenote number={23}>Palme 2005.</Sidenote>

So Sweden is not a simple counter-example to Beveridge's conditioned universalism. It shows that the dependent-wife category was a British design choice, not a mathematical necessity — and that configuring the system differently is not the same as escaping the problem. ATP's standard person was a worker with a continuous, full-time, adequately paid thirty-year career: a fiction, like Beveridge's breadwinner, but a different one, disadvantaging those who fell short of continuous full-time work rather than those outside formal work altogether. Sweden relocated the gap between the model person and actual lives, narrowing it for some women and barely moving it for others. The arithmetic of welfare is political before it is technical. Who counts as the standard person, which kinds of work the records can see, and what fraction of income the formula protects are fixed not by mathematics but by the settlements within which mathematics is put to work. In Stockholm and London, they were fixed differently.

## 5. The Statistical Infrastructure: Building the Machinery of Governance

Beveridge's welfare state needed answers that were statistical as much as moral. Could contributions of 4 shillings and 3 pence a week sustain projected claims? Were flat-rate benefits calibrated against Rowntree still adequate once prices rose and households diverged from the model? Was poverty falling? Answering at national scale required data that had never been systematically collected — household incomes, spending, employment histories, living conditions — that could be held against the system's own targets. In 1942 that infrastructure did not exist. It had to be built.

Some of it was already under construction. In January 1941 Churchill instructed the Cabinet Secretary, Sir Edward Bridges, to set up a central statistical office so that departments would work from agreed figures, and Harry Campion became its first Director. Its first task was wartime resource allocation, but its logic was Beveridge's: governing at scale required standardised, centrally produced figures whose authority came from consistency rather than any official's judgment.<sup aria-describedby="sn-24">24</sup> The Central Statistical Office's first major achievement was the national income and expenditure accounts — the annual *Blue Book*. GDP is not a discovery but a set of conventions: market transactions count as production and unpaid domestic labour does not; government spending is final demand, not investment in social reproduction. Worked out in the 1940s alongside Keynes's wartime national income accounting, those conventions have framed every later argument about what "the national income can afford." When Claus Moser became Director in 1967 with a mandate to integrate economic and social statistics, the first *Social Trends* (1970) made British social life as coherent an object of government as economic activity.

<Sidenote number={24}>War Cabinet, *Establishment of the Central Statistical Office* (1941).</Sidenote>

### The survey that refuted its owner

The Family Expenditure Survey, continuous from 1957, began with a mundane need: the Retail Price Index required current weights for how households actually spent. Some 7,000 to 10,000 households a year kept two-week diaries of every purchase — bread, mortgage payments, cinema tickets, coal — coded and aggregated into the basket whose price movements defined official inflation.<sup aria-describedby="sn-25">25</sup> It was built for price statistics, not poverty measurement. What it produced, incidentally, was the first systematic population-level picture of household consumption across income deciles, household types, regions, and years.

<Sidenote number={25}>Ministry of Labour, *Family Expenditure Survey 1957: First Report*.</Sidenote>

Brian Abel-Smith and Peter Townsend saw what was in the data before the welfare state's administrators did. Using Ministry of Labour expenditure inquiries, they set a poverty line at 1.4 times National Assistance scales, applied to equivalised household expenditure. *The Poor and the Poorest* (1965) found that poverty had not been abolished; it had persisted and, by their measure, grown.<sup aria-describedby="sn-26">26</sup> The welfare state had generated the data that refuted its own claims. The Central Statistical Office had not meant to equip Townsend to embarrass the government. It had meant to improve the price index. The data's political uses followed from what it could do, not what it was built to do.

<Sidenote number={26}>Abel-Smith and Townsend, *The Poor and the Poorest* (1965).</Sidenote>

The General Household Survey, from 1971, extended the reach to employment, pensions, education, health, smoking and drinking, family composition, income, and housing, interviewing every adult in some nine to ten thousand households a year.<sup aria-describedby="sn-27">27</sup> Over the following decades it documented a transformation the Beveridge model had not anticipated: average household size falling from 2.91 persons in 1971, one-person households nearly doubling, lone-parent families tripling. The breadwinner, dependent wife, and two children of the actuarial tables were becoming a smaller share of the population the system had to serve, and the survey made the shift visible decade by decade.

<Sidenote number={27}>Office of Population Censuses and Surveys, *The General Household Survey: Introductory Report* (1973).</Sidenote>

### Legible, and persistent

In Scott's terms, the national accounts, the two surveys, and the contribution records together built a grid through which the welfare state could see its population — not the person with her particular rent and health, but the statistical household, the income decile, the employment band, the family type. The grid made redistribution administrable: you could ask whether benefits matched the cost of living because the Family Expenditure Survey told you what living cost, and project pension liabilities because the General Household Survey told you how many households sat in each bracket. The same grid made the population governable in ways redistribution did not require. Its categories — household composition, employment status, income band, contribution record — outlasted the settlement that produced them. The household the survey measured became the household benefit rules would regulate; the employment status it tracked became the status conditionality regimes would police; the income band became what means-testing would interrogate.

None of this was built to create an automated poorhouse. It was built to know whether poverty was falling, whether benefits were adequate, whether contributions could sustain claims — defensible purposes that it served. What it could not control was what its categories and data flows would be used for once the political settlement that produced them was dismantled. The machinery of governance is not politically neutral. It is politically persistent. The categories created to make redistribution possible survived the redistribution and went to work for something else.

## 6. The Scottish Settlement: Differential Application and Its Limits

Scotland did not have one relationship to Beveridge's model. It had two. In the industrial belt — Glasgow, Motherwell, Dundee, the mining villages of Fife and Lanarkshire, the Clyde shipyards — the man working full-time in one heavy industry, stamped weekly by one employer, came closer to the system's standard person than almost anywhere in Britain. In the crofting counties — Sutherland, Ross and Cromarty, Inverness, the Western Isles — the standard person did not exist. Work there was seasonal, mixed, partly unwaged, and spread across several sources of income in ways the actuarial model could not track. The same national formula landed differently in the two Scotlands, and the difference was not random.

### A different Poor Law

Scotland had run its own poor relief system for a century before Beveridge. The Poor Law (Scotland) Act 1845 created the Board of Supervision for the Relief of the Poor, based in Edinburgh, to oversee reformed parochial boards.<sup aria-describedby="sn-28">28</sup> It differed from the English New Poor Law in two ways. It did not insist on the workhouse test: outdoor relief in cash or kind was common, especially in rural parishes where a poorhouse was out of reach and the parish knew its applicants by name. And the able-bodied unemployed had no legal right to relief until the 1920s. Relief was for those disabled by age or infirmity, widows and deserted wives with children, and orphans; the man who simply could not find work had no claim on the Scottish parish.

<Sidenote number={28}>Poor Law Amendment (Scotland) Act 1845.</Sidenote>

The Board's minute books, held in the National Records of Scotland as the HH23 series, record a century of governing through local judgment and case-by-case negotiation: parochial boards bargaining with Edinburgh over poorhouse designs, disputing which parish was liable for a pauper, answering complaints from applicants refused or inadequately relieved. Between 1855 and 1865 the Board spent nearly ten years revising Arbroath's plans for a poorhouse before approving a combination with neighbouring St Vigeans under pressure from ratepayers. In 1871 it investigated the death of a pauper, Alexander Macdonald, in which the local Inspector of Poor was implicated, and issued a severe reprimand short of dismissal.<sup aria-describedby="sn-29">29</sup> Its grammar was entirely different from the actuarial model that would replace it.

<Sidenote number={29}>Records of the Board of Supervision for the Relief of the Poor in Scotland, National Records of Scotland, HH23.</Sidenote>

The National Assistance Act 1948 abolished the Scottish Poor Law along with the English. But county and burgh councils, which had run public assistance since the Local Government (Scotland) Act 1929, became the local nodes of the new national system, and officials who had learned their trade under the old one carried forward its assumptions about who deserved support, its informal assessments of household circumstance, and its local knowledge of who could and could not be pressed. The new system was layered on the old rather than replacing it cleanly.

### Clydeside: the best fit, and the most exposed

The industrial Lowlands absorbed the Beveridge model most readily, and that was a source of future vulnerability. National Insurance was designed for economies like Clydeside's and Lanarkshire's: large employers, regular contracts, male-dominated industries, clean contribution records. When those industries contracted from the 1960s and collapsed in the 1980s, the communities whose welfare had been most thoroughly organised around the breadwinner's contribution record lost not only their incomes but their structural eligibility for the contributory benefits they had been promised. The system's most committed subscribers became its most dependent claimants within a generation. Its universalism had always been conditional on the industries continuing to employ the men whose records it counted on.

### The crofting counties: a structural mismatch

In the crofting counties the mismatch was structural from the start. The Crofters Holdings (Scotland) Act 1886, passed in the aftermath of the Clearances and later called a "Magna Carta" for Highland small tenants, created a distinct legal tenure in seven counties: Shetland, Orkney, Caithness, Sutherland, Ross and Cromarty, Inverness, and Argyll.<sup aria-describedby="sn-30">30</sup> Crofts were small holdings, typically five hectares or less, with shared common grazings, and never sufficient alone. Households combined cultivation and livestock with seasonal estate or farm labour, fishing, kelp, and later local wage work and public-service jobs. By the 2007 crofting survey, only around 30 per cent of crofting households' income came from crofting itself; in 2022 crofting activity generated an average of about £4,538 per crofter a year.<sup aria-describedby="sn-31">31</sup> That was not a failure of crofting. It was its nature.

<Sidenote number={30}>Crofters Holdings (Scotland) Act 1886.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={31}>Crofting Commission, *Annual Report and Accounts 2021–22*.</Sidenote>

National Insurance assumed a Class I employee whose employer stamped a card every week. A crofter who also worked seasonally in local construction was an employee for part of the year and self-employed on the croft for the rest. The self-employed paid flat-rate Class 2 contributions and were entitled to a basic retirement pension but not to unemployment benefit — the contingency seasonal work made most relevant. Unemployment benefit itself was built around a "full normal extent" of employment, and was not payable for days on which a worker "does not in the normal course expect to be working."<sup aria-describedby="sn-32">32</sup> For workers whose normal pattern was precisely not full-year, full-time work, the rule was a systematic exclusion in actuarial language. The formula did not distinguish between a Glasgow shipyard worker temporarily laid off and a Harris crofter at the end of the fishing season. It noted that one had a full record and a normal working pattern, and the other did not.

<Sidenote number={32}>House of Commons, debate on National Insurance for seasonal and agricultural workers (1960).</Sidenote>

The crofting counties were also statistically thin. The postwar poverty studies that shaped welfare debate drew on the Family Expenditure Survey, whose national sample of seven to ten thousand households left crofting communities represented by a handful of cases in any year.<sup aria-describedby="sn-33">33</sup> High transport costs, reliance on non-cash production, poor housing, and distance from services were too thinly sampled to shape national poverty lines or benefit adequacy. In a 1968 House of Lords debate on the Highlands, speakers described seven crofting counties covering nine million acres, with chronic underemployment and steady out-migration of the young, made worse by national policies — the Selective Employment Tax, transport cuts, the absence of industrial investment.<sup aria-describedby="sn-34">34</sup> The welfare state reached these communities mainly through means-tested National Assistance and later Supplementary Benefit: the residual system Beveridge had designed as a minimal last resort became the main support for the people his universal system could not reach. That was the structural consequence of applying a formula designed for one kind of economy to a country with two, and assuming the formula's standard person would be universal enough to serve both.

<Sidenote number={34}>House of Lords, debate on the Highlands and Islands (1968).</Sidenote>

## 7. Cracks in the Foundation: The Return of Means-Testing

National Assistance, the means-tested net that replaced the Poor Law, was meant to be residual in the strict sense: a temporary provision for the few whose needs could not be met by contributory insurance or by the household incomes full employment would generate. Beveridge opposed means tests and designed National Insurance to make them unnecessary.<sup aria-describedby="sn-35">35</sup> Assistance would exist, but it would serve very few people and would not define the welfare state's character.

<Sidenote number={35}>Institute for Fiscal Studies, *Means-Testing and the Legacy of the Beveridge Settlement* (2023).</Sidenote>

It did not stay residual. Harold Wilson's Labour government responded by improving the safety net rather than questioning the contributory model's adequacy. The Ministry of Social Security Act 1966 replaced National Assistance with Supplementary Benefit, run by a new Supplementary Benefits Commission. The change was substantive: statutory entitlement replaced discretionary relief; scale rates rose roughly 27 per cent above October 1964 National Assistance rates, against an 8.8 per cent rise in the Retail Price Index; and a government advertising campaign actively recruited claimants for the first time.<sup aria-describedby="sn-36">36</sup> Richard Titmuss, whose institutional/residual distinction had supplied the vocabulary for defending universalism against means-testing, became the Commission's deputy chairman. The author of the critique was given the task of civilising what he had critiqued — an accurate measure of the distance between having the right analytical framework and having the political means to stop the system it described from growing.

<Sidenote number={36}>Ministry of Social Security Act 1966 and related parliamentary proceedings.</Sidenote>

### Paying people who work

The Family Income Supplement, paid from 1971, opened a new front. It topped up the wages of low-paid full-time workers with children: half the gap between gross earnings and a prescribed threshold, awarded for 52 weeks regardless of later changes in income.<sup aria-describedby="sn-37">37</sup> This was a departure from Beveridge's architecture at its foundations. His model assumed that full-time, regular, insured employment would keep a household above the subsistence floor, with the state stepping in only when work was interrupted. The supplement conceded that full-time work could pay poverty wages — that the labour market itself was producing the need the welfare state was meant to prevent.

<Sidenote number={37}>Family Income Supplements Act.</Sidenote>

The vocabulary shifted before the architecture did. In Beveridge's framework the question was whether a person had contributed enough. In the New Right's reframing of the 1970s and 1980s it became whether a person was making enough effort to work, and whether benefits rewarded or undermined that effort. "Deserving" and "undeserving," which the actuarial model had been meant to displace, returned as "incentives" and "targeting" — the same sorting of people by their relationship to the labour market, now in the language of efficiency rather than morality, with the second group's support made conditional, stigmatised, and inadequate.

### 1986: the architecture of the trap

The Social Security Act 1986, implemented in April 1988, built that reframing into the system. Income Support replaced Supplementary Benefit, with a stricter definition of "remunerative work": anyone working 24 hours a week or more was excluded and directed to Family Credit if they had children, or to no income-related support if they did not. Family Credit paid a maximum credit withdrawn at 70 per cent of net income above a threshold.<sup aria-describedby="sn-38">38</sup> Combined with income tax and National Insurance, effective marginal withdrawal rates could exceed 90 per cent in some income bands: a household gaining £10 in gross earnings could keep less than £1 of it. The poverty trap was not a malfunction. It was the system's topology — a structure of means-tests, tapers, and thresholds in which it was arithmetically hard to raise net income by earning more. Housing Benefit, formalised alongside, completed an income-related architecture whose basic shape, the Institute for Fiscal Studies concluded, set the structure of UK means-tested support until Universal Credit.<sup aria-describedby="sn-39">39</sup>

<Sidenote number={38}>Social Security Act 1986.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={39}>Institute for Fiscal Studies 2023.</Sidenote>

The same Act moved against the contributory model's most ambitious component. The State Earnings-Related Pension Scheme, created by the Social Security Pensions Act 1975 as a limited British answer to ATP, was cut back — a less generous accrual formula, a longer reference period — and employees were offered an extra National Insurance rebate to contract out into personal pensions.<sup aria-describedby="sn-40">40</sup> Presented as choice and sustainability, it was also a decision to make the public system less generous for middle earners and means-tested support more likely at both ends of working life. The 1986 Act was less an assault on universalism than a reorganisation of risk: more of it borne privately, more of the residue means-tested at the bottom.

<Sidenote number={40}>Social Security Act 1986.</Sidenote>

### Encoded, not abolished

By 1988 the contribution record had not insulated the population from means-testing. The wage stop was gone, but the taper had taken up its logic. The cohabitation rule survived in attenuated form in Income Support's household aggregation. The conditions Beveridge attached to universalism had been extended, refined, and computerised. Income Support, Family Credit, and Housing Benefit were rule-based calculations — national scale rates, standard disregards, detailed regulations on income and capital — that suited computers precisely because they had converted "does this person deserve relief?" into "does this household's income fall below this threshold, given these parameters?" Beveridge designed his system to make the Poor Law's question redundant. The growth of income-tested assistance shows that the question did not disappear. It returned in a new grammar of household resources, scale rates, hours rules, and tapers. The postwar settlement abolished much of the Poor Law's arbitrariness and stigma. Its residual systems kept open a route by which need could again be investigated, classified, and made conditional. The moral assessment had not been abolished. It had been encoded.

### Connection Forward

Chapter 4 asks what was simultaneously present and defeated in the same decades: a materially specific, household-grounded counter-mathematics to the actuarial model, built from grocery receipts, welfare rights campaigns, and deprivation surveys. Understanding why that alternative lost requires understanding what the postwar welfare state had already built and why its administrative logic so consistently resolved toward simplification.
