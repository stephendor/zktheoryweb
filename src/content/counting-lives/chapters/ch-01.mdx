## Synopsis

Mollie Orshansky did not publish a poverty line. In January 1965 she published a schedule of 124 thresholds, each derived from the cost of the US Department of Agriculture's cheapest food plan for a particular kind of household, multiplied by the share of income that the 1955 Household Food Consumption Survey showed families actually spent on food. The method had four properties that were at once mathematical and political: its inputs were published, so it was transparent; its arithmetic was one step, so it was contestable; it was anchored in what households ate, so it was materially grounded; and it distinguished a farm couple in their seventies from a city family of eight, so it was granular. Those properties also made it harder to administer. In 1969 the Bureau of the Budget made the thresholds the federal government's statistical definition of poverty and moved their annual updating onto the Consumer Price Index; in 1981 the detailed matrix was reduced to 48 cells. Each step bought a more stable series at the cost of the question Orshansky had built into it — whether the number still described what a household needed. The official US thresholds still descend from her calculation. That question is the one this chapter asks of them, and of Universal Credit's standard allowances today.

## 1. The Kitchen as Laboratory

We do not know what Mollie Orshansky's childhood kitchen looked like. We do not know the colour of the walls, or whether the table was wood or tin, or how many chairs there were for how many children. We know that she was born in New York City in 1915, the daughter of immigrants from what is now Ukraine, that she and her sisters grew up poor, and that her father worked several jobs.<sup aria-describedby="sn-1">1</sup> The terms of that poverty were material: groceries, rent, and the number of bodies a wage had to feed. Her later work made those terms the basis of a federal statistic.

<Sidenote number={1}>Fisher, "Remembering Mollie Orshansky — The Developer of the Poverty Thresholds" (Social Security Administration).</Sidenote>

At a kitchen table the question is not "what is the poverty rate?" but "what can we eat tonight and still pay the rent this week?" Orshansky began with a household and asked what it needed to survive. Could this household, of this size, with these members, feed itself adequately on this income? In "Children of the Poor" she put the choice in the household's own terms: a family might have to choose between "an adequate diet at minimum cost" and another necessity.<sup aria-describedby="sn-2">2</sup>

<Sidenote number={2}>Orshansky, "Children of the Poor," *Social Security Bulletin* (July 1963), p. 3.</Sidenote>

### The discipline the profession forgot

That orientation has a disciplinary home. Home economics was what Carolyn Goldstein calls "an applied science," built on the proposition that the household was a measurable economic unit — that the food a family ate, the calories it required, and the budget it balanced or failed to balance were as amenable to rigorous analysis as anything in the economics department down the hall. David Philippy is blunter: "Home economics is a neglected field in the history of economics."<sup aria-describedby="sn-3">3</sup> The neglect tracked gender with uncomfortable precision. The economists building abstract models of markets and rational choice were overwhelmingly men; the researchers measuring food baskets, caloric requirements, and household budgets were overwhelmingly women, and the profession funded them accordingly. Yet for the question of whether a household could live on its income, home economics held the instruments. Classical economics could place a household in the bottom quintile of the income distribution. It could not, by itself, say whether that household could feed itself.

<Sidenote number={3}>Goldstein, *Creating Consumers* (2014), p. 39; Philippy, "Ellen Richards's Home Economics Movement and the Birth of the Economics of Consumption" (2021), p. 379.</Sidenote>

Orshansky graduated from Hunter College in 1935 with a degree in mathematics and statistics, not home economics.<sup aria-describedby="sn-4">4</sup> But Hunter's home economics tradition is the institutional context for the method she would build, and Margaret G. Reid's *Economics of Household Production* (1934) gives its clearest statement — not as a documented influence on Orshansky, but as a framework for reading her problem. Household production, Reid wrote, consists of "those unpaid activities" that "might be replaced by market goods, or paid services."<sup aria-describedby="sn-5">5</sup> Cooking, care, and household management determine what an income can provide, even though they leave no wage record. Orshansky's thresholds share that premise: each described a specific household type — a single person over 65, a couple with two children under 6, a family of eight — because what a household needs depends on who is in it.

<Sidenote number={4}>Fisher, "Remembering Mollie Orshansky."</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={5}>Reid, *Economics of Household Production* (1934), p. 11.</Sidenote>

### The epistemological inversion

A population statistic typically begins by distributing people along a common scale and asking what pattern emerges. Orshansky's thresholds began with a household's food budget and asked whether its income could meet it. The food budget came first; the threshold followed. At every stage the measure was anchored in a published figure: the cost of the USDA's Economy Food Plan for a household of a given size and composition, the food-to-income ratio observed in the 1955 survey, the difference between farm and non-farm food costs.<sup aria-describedby="sn-6">6</sup> Remove the specificity and the measure collapses. That was the point.

<Sidenote number={6}>Orshansky, "Counting the Poor: Another Look at the Poverty Profile," *Social Security Bulletin* (January 1965), pp. 3–8; Fisher, *The Development of the Orshansky Poverty Thresholds and Their Subsequent History as the Official U.S. Poverty Measure* (1997).</Sidenote>

Ian Hacking's account of "making up people" explains why the form of the measure matters: human classifications and the people classified change one another.<sup aria-describedby="sn-7">7</sup> Orshansky's 124 thresholds made up households whose poverty could be described with precision — households that could, in principle, contest their classification by pointing at a grocery receipt and saying "this is wrong." Theodore Porter supplies the other half. Quantification in public life, he argues, gains authority from impersonal mechanical rules that make decisions look objective.<sup aria-describedby="sn-8">8</sup> Orshansky's matrix is Porter's counter-case: its inputs were published, anyone could say precisely where they disagreed, and it rested on household consumption rather than a fiscal target. Those properties made it scientifically stronger and administratively inconvenient. The kitchen knew something a statistical office could easily lose: poverty is particular. The question was whether an institution that needed standard measures could keep it visible.

<Sidenote number={7}>Hacking, "Making Up People" (1986).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={8}>Porter, *Trust in Numbers* (1995).</Sidenote>

## 2. From Beveridge to the Poverty Line: The Postwar Demand for a Number

The postwar welfare state could count contributors, claimants, and expenditure without measuring whether any particular household had enough to live on. William Beveridge's *Social Insurance and Allied Services* (1942) built a system around the question "who is insured?" rather than "who is poor?" Its flat-rate benefits — 24 shillings a week for a single adult, 40 for a married couple — came from political judgment and actuarial constraint, not from a household-by-household inquiry into food, rent, fuel, and clothing.<sup aria-describedby="sn-9">9</sup> The scheme could tell the Treasury how many people were claiming and what it cost. It could not tell anyone whether a family of four in York or a retired woman in Stepney had enough to eat.

<Sidenote number={9}>Beveridge, *Social Insurance and Allied Services* (1942); Harris, *William Beveridge: A Biography* (1997).</Sidenote>

The report also concealed a collaboration. Ann Oakley's archival work recovers Janet Philip's editorial and research contribution, which the published document does not name; Beveridge later acknowledged that "it would be idle to separate what came from her mind and what from his."<sup aria-describedby="sn-10">10</sup> Philip's prose was absorbed into the report; Orshansky's household distinctions would later be pared from the official series. In both cases the public instrument hides the work that made it possible.

<Sidenote number={10}>Oakley, *Forgotten Wives: How Women Get Written Out of History* (2023).</Sidenote>

The American problem was different: there was no official federal poverty measure at all. Bureau of Labor Statistics family budgets and experimental lines inside the Social Security Administration existed, but no programme had adopted any of them.<sup aria-describedby="sn-11">11</sup> In 1963, Walter Heller's Council of Economic Advisers used a single \$3,000 annual family income for its national poverty count — a figure that did not vary by household size or composition.<sup aria-describedby="sn-12">12</sup> Orshansky objected that one threshold for all family sizes systematically understated the number of children in poverty relative to elderly people. A national yardstick could count people. It could not distinguish the circumstances in which they lived.

<Sidenote number={11}>Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={12}>Heller, Council of Economic Advisers (1963); Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

In July 1963 Orshansky published "Children of the Poor," using household food costs to construct an income standard for families with children. Two years later she described the exercise plainly: not a final statement of adequate income, but a way to say "how much, on an average, is too little."<sup aria-describedby="sn-13">13</sup> For the first time, a federal statistician had supplied a household-specific measure of poverty, and its arithmetic began with the cost of feeding a particular family.

<Sidenote number={13}>Orshansky 1963; Orshansky 1965, p. 3.</Sidenote>

## 3. The Matrices: Transparency as a Mathematical Property

What Orshansky published in January 1965 was a schedule of 124 thresholds. She stated the principle directly: the cash income required changes between "the family of two and the family of eight," and between metropolitan and farm residence.<sup aria-describedby="sn-14">14</sup> A poverty rate divides a population in two. A threshold schedule differentiates within it, preserving the fact that poverty in a two-person elderly household on an Iowa farm is not the same as poverty in a household of eight in a Philadelphia row house. The rate asks how many. The schedule asks who, specifically, and under what material conditions.

<Sidenote number={14}>Orshansky 1965, p. 3; Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

### The method: from food to income

The calculation has three steps, and each rests on a published observation.

**A food budget.** The USDA published four tiered food plans — Economy, Low-Cost, Moderate-Cost, and Liberal — each a costed basket of specific foods in specific quantities, priced in retail markets and calculated for households of different size and composition.<sup aria-describedby="sn-15">15</sup> Orshansky chose the Economy plan, the cheapest, which she described as intended for "temporary or emergency use when funds are low."<sup aria-describedby="sn-16">16</sup> The choice was deliberate: a floor so austere that no critic could dismiss the people below it as improvident.

<Sidenote number={15}>USDA, Economy Food Plan (1961 methodology).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={16}>Orshansky 1965, p. 5.</Sidenote>

**A ratio.** The 1955 Household Food Consumption Survey found that families of three or more spent about one-third of their after-tax income on food.<sup aria-describedby="sn-17">17</sup> This was not a claim about what families should spend, but an observation about what they did.

<Sidenote number={17}>USDA, *Household Food Consumption Survey, 1955*, Report No. 1.</Sidenote>

**An inversion.** If food is a third of income, the minimum income a household needs is three times its minimum food budget. For a family of two adults and two young children, that came to \$3,100 a year off the farm and \$1,860 on it.<sup aria-describedby="sn-18">18</sup> A household that could not afford three times the minimum food basket could not, by the survey's own evidence, eat at that minimum and still pay for rent, fuel, clothing, transport, and medical care.

<Sidenote number={18}>Orshansky 1965, pp. 3, 8; Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

### Five dimensions, four properties

The schedule distinguished households by size, the age of the household head, the number of children, the sex of the head, and farm residence — each a material fact about how households lived. Farm families grew some of their own food, so Orshansky set farm thresholds at 60 per cent of the non-farm figures; the Office of Economic Opportunity raised that to 70 per cent in 1965, the 1969 revision to 85 per cent, and in 1981 the non-farm thresholds were applied to everyone.<sup aria-describedby="sn-19">19</sup> The child dimension mattered most politically: "Children of the Poor" carried that title because the \$3,000 line treated two adults and two adults with four children as the same household. The matrix made children visible in the poverty count. The single line hid them.

<Sidenote number={19}>USDA 1961; Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

Four properties followed. **Transparency**: the food plan, the survey, and the multiplication were public, and anyone with a library and a pencil could recalculate a threshold from scratch. **Contestability**: a nutritionist could name the foods she would add, an economist could cite later survey data showing the food share had fallen — a contestable measure distributes the power to challenge it rather than concentrating the power to assert it. **Material grounding**: the threshold was derived from something a household had to do, eat, at a level the government's own nutritionists set as the minimum; it could be wrong, but checkably rather than arbitrarily. **Granularity**: a measure that treats poverty as one thing has already decided not to see the differences that matter most to the people living them. These were the properties Orshansky built, and they were the ones that made her schedule hard to administer.

## 4. Political Battles and the Cost of Complexity

Heller needed a number that could mobilise political will. Orshansky needed a measure that could tell which children were poor. The line counted heads. The matrix counted mouths.

The Office of Economic Opportunity adopted Orshansky's thresholds in May 1965 as its working definition of poverty, and the Census Bureau began using them soon after. But different agencies adjusted them differently, producing inconsistent counts that embarrassed the administration.<sup aria-describedby="sn-20">20</sup> On 29 August 1969 the Bureau of the Budget directed federal agencies to use the revised Census thresholds for statistical purposes, making them the government's official definition of poverty. The directive did not replace the matrix with one national figure. It formalised two revisions.

<Sidenote number={20}>Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

The first was CPI indexing. Until 1969 the thresholds moved each year with the cost of the Economy Food Plan. In 1968 the SSA had proposed revised food plans based on 1965 survey data, and a higher multiplier from the same survey was under discussion; together they would have raised the thresholds by 25 to 30 per cent. The interagency committee instead applied the Consumer Price Index to the 1963 thresholds. A price index can reproduce a series indefinitely without ever testing whether the food budget, or the food-to-income ratio, still describes what households need; revisiting either became a separate policy decision the routine no longer required anyone to make. The second revision narrowed the farm differential. The sex-of-head distinction survived until 1981, when the matrix was cut to 48 cells and the separate thresholds for male- and female-headed households were averaged into one.

Orshansky did not object to a consistent series. She objected to consistency standing in for a renewed judgment about need. In a 1970 newspaper interview she said the 1969 decision "tends to freeze the poverty line despite changes in buying habits and changes in acceptable living standards."<sup aria-describedby="sn-21">21</sup> A threshold can be stable enough to compare across years while becoming steadily less adequate as an account of what households require. An alternative was available: in 1979 she and Carol Fendler set out an updated measure based on the USDA's Thrifty Food Plan and a multiplier of 3.4 derived from the 1965 survey.<sup aria-describedby="sn-22">22</sup> Once a fixed CPI rule governed the threshold's movement, the question of whether it still fitted household life had no place in its routine.

<Sidenote number={21}>Quoted in Fisher 1997, p. 28.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={22}>Fendler and Orshansky, "Improving the Poverty Definition" (1979); Fisher 1997, p. 42.</Sidenote>

## 5. Thresholds as Governance, Then and Now

The US poverty measure now has two related forms. The Census Bureau's official thresholds are a statistical yardstick: they vary by family size and composition, not by place, and are updated each year using CPI-U.<sup aria-describedby="sn-23">23</sup> The Department of Health and Human Services' poverty guidelines are a simplified administrative version of them, used by Medicaid and other programmes to determine eligibility.<sup aria-describedby="sn-24">24</sup> Both descend from Orshansky's method. The threshold is a fossil in a precise sense: its movement is governed by a price index rather than by any reassessment of the food plan, the multiplier, and the household distinctions from which it was derived. That preserves a comparable series. It does not answer whether the series still represents the conditions it is meant to measure.

<Sidenote number={23}>US Census Bureau, "How the Census Bureau Measures Poverty."</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={24}>HHS, "Annual Update of the HHS Poverty Guidelines" (2026).</Sidenote>

Since 2011 the Census Bureau has also published the Supplemental Poverty Measure, which builds its threshold from five years of spending on food, clothing, shelter, and utilities, adjusts it for local housing costs, counts noncash benefits, and subtracts taxes, work expenses, and medical costs.<sup aria-describedby="sn-25">25</sup> These are not neutral corrections; they redefine what counts as a resource and an expense. But HHS's 2026 guidelines are still derived from the official thresholds, not the SPM. The better-grounded measure sits beside the administrative series without choosing the number that decides who qualifies for Medicaid.

<Sidenote number={25}>US Census Bureau, *The Supplemental Poverty Measure: 2011*; HHS 2026.</Sidenote>

### Universal Credit's standard allowances

In Britain, the Department for Work and Pensions publishes Universal Credit's standard allowances as a rate table. For 2026–27 the monthly amounts are £338.58 for a single claimant under 25, £424.90 for a single claimant aged 25 or over, £528.34 for joint claimants both under 25, and £666.97 where one or both are 25 or over.<sup aria-describedby="sn-26">26</sup> They rose by more than inflation in April 2026: the Universal Credit Act 2025 requires the standard allowance to increase each year to 2029–30 by CPI plus a fixed statutory uplift, 2.3 per cent for 2026–27.<sup aria-describedby="sn-27">27</sup>

<Sidenote number={26}>DWP, *Benefit and Pension Rates 2026 to 2027*.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={27}>Universal Credit Act 2025 (c. 22).</Sidenote>

The rate table makes the size of an award public. It does not show how the levels were set or whether they match what a household's life costs — and the 2025 Act raises the allowance by a price index plus a percentage written into statute, not by a published account of what a single adult or a couple needs to eat, heat a home, and stay clothed. The comparison with Orshansky is not a claim of transatlantic descent; Universal Credit has its own history. It is a comparison of the evidence each record puts in front of a reader. Her 1965 article set out a food plan, a multiplier, and thresholds differentiated by household type. The DWP table states amounts. Only one of them gives a critic the materials to argue about adequacy on the measure's own terms.

What Orshansky left was not a timeless line but a method whose assumptions remained open to revision — a standard for the work itself. A poverty measure can be transparent about its inputs, differentiated by household circumstances, and open to revision when the evidence changes. A stable series has its uses. It cannot relieve the institutions that run it of the duty to decide whether its inherited assumptions still describe what households need.

### Connection Forward

Chapter 2 turns to the statistical tradition Orshansky was working against: the tools Galton and Pearson developed not to describe human variation but to rank it, and whose grammar — the normal curve, the correlation coefficient, the z-score — became so embedded in quantitative social science that its eugenic origins disappeared from view. The kitchen table where Orshansky began is not simply a counter-image to Galton's laboratory in South Kensington. It is a methodological counter-claim: to measure what households need rather than where they rank in a distribution.
