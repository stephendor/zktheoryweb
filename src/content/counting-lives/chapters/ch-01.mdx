## Synopsis

Mollie Orshansky did not publish a poverty line. In January 1965 she published a schedule of 124 thresholds, each derived from the cost of the US Department of Agriculture's cheapest food plan for a particular kind of household, multiplied by the share of income that the 1955 Household Food Consumption Survey showed families actually spent on food. The method had four properties that were at once mathematical and political: its inputs were published, so it was transparent; its arithmetic was one step, so it was contestable; it was anchored in what households ate, so it was materially grounded; and it distinguished a farm couple in their seventies from a city family of eight, so it was granular. Those same properties made it harder to administer. In 1969 the Bureau of the Budget made the thresholds the federal government's statistical definition of poverty and moved their annual updating onto the Consumer Price Index; in 1981 the detailed matrix was reduced to 48 cells. Each step bought a more stable series at the cost of the question Orshansky had built into it — whether the number still described what a household needed. The official US thresholds still descend from her calculation. The question she kept open is the one this chapter asks of them, and of Universal Credit's standard allowances today.

## 1. The Kitchen as Laboratory

We do not know what Mollie Orshansky's childhood kitchen looked like. We do not know the colour of the walls, or whether the table was wood or tin, or how many chairs there were for how many children. We know that she was born in New York City in 1915, the daughter of immigrants from what is now Ukraine, that she and her sisters grew up poor, and that her father worked several jobs.<sup aria-describedby="sn-1">1</sup> The terms of that poverty were material: groceries, rent, and the number of bodies a wage had to feed. Her later work made those terms the basis of a federal statistic. Poverty, in her measure, was a condition of actual households with actual stomachs, not a position in a distribution.

<Sidenote number={1}>Fisher, "Remembering Mollie Orshansky — The Developer of the Poverty Thresholds" (Social Security Administration).</Sidenote>

At a kitchen table the question is not "what is the poverty rate?" but "what can we eat tonight and still pay the rent this week?" Orshansky began with a household and asked what it needed to survive. The unit of analysis was the family; the threshold was an income standard derived from a food budget; the test was material. Could this household, of this size, with these members, feed itself adequately on this income? In "Children of the Poor" she put the choice in the household's own terms: a family might have to choose between "an adequate diet at minimum cost" and another necessity.<sup aria-describedby="sn-2">2</sup>

<Sidenote number={2}>Orshansky, "Children of the Poor," *Social Security Bulletin* (July 1963), p. 3.</Sidenote>

### The discipline the profession forgot

That orientation has a disciplinary home. Home economics was what Carolyn Goldstein calls "an applied science," built on the proposition that the household was a measurable economic unit — that the food a family ate, the calories it required, the budget it balanced or failed to balance, and the labour it performed were as amenable to rigorous analysis as anything studied in the economics department down the hall. David Philippy is blunter: "Home economics is a neglected field in the history of economics," though its applied work gave economists "substantial empirical materials and new insights into family budgets and consumption behavior."<sup aria-describedby="sn-3">3</sup> At Hunter College, the field ran from an 1888 course in food composition to a full major in 1937 and a master's in nutrition in 1957 — a record of steady professionalisation that conceals one of steady marginalisation.

<Sidenote number={3}>Goldstein, *Creating Consumers* (2014), p. 39; Philippy, "Ellen Richards's Home Economics Movement and the Birth of the Economics of Consumption" (2021), p. 379; Hunter College, *Home Economics in a Liberal Arts College*.</Sidenote>

The marginalisation tracked gender with uncomfortable precision. The economists building abstract models of markets and rational choice were overwhelmingly men; the researchers measuring food baskets, caloric requirements, and household budgets were overwhelmingly women. The profession treated the first as serious science and the second as domestic craft in academic dress, and funded them accordingly. Yet for the particular problem of whether a household could live on its income, home economics held the instruments: food composition tables, nutritional minimum standards, household budget surveys. Classical economics could place a household in the bottom quintile of the income distribution. It could not, by itself, say whether that household could feed itself.

Orshansky graduated from Hunter in 1935 with a degree in mathematics and statistics, not home economics — the major began two years later.<sup aria-describedby="sn-4">4</sup> But Hunter's home economics tradition is the institutional context for the method she would build: it made the material conditions of households a legitimate object of systematic study. Her later choices — the household as the unit of analysis, an income threshold derived from a food budget, and a matrix differentiated by household size and composition — share that orientation.

<Sidenote number={4}>Fisher, "Remembering Mollie Orshansky."</Sidenote>

### Margaret Reid and the household as an economic object

Margaret G. Reid's *Economics of Household Production* (1934) gives the clearest account of the household as a site of production as well as consumption. It belongs here as a framework for reading the problem Orshansky addressed, not as a documented influence on her. Household production, Reid wrote, consists of "those unpaid activities" that "might be replaced by market goods, or paid services."<sup aria-describedby="sn-5">5</sup> Cooking, care, and household management are not private background to economic life; they determine what an income can actually provide, even though they leave no wage record.

<Sidenote number={5}>Reid, *Economics of Household Production* (1934), p. 11.</Sidenote>

The affinity with Orshansky is structural. Her thresholds treat material adequacy as an empirical question about whether a particular arrangement of people can be fed, sheltered, and sustained. Each threshold described a specific household type — a single person over 65, a couple with two children under 6, a family of eight with a head under 35 — because what a household needs depends on who is in it.<sup aria-describedby="sn-6">6</sup> A measure that ignores this is measuring something else.

<Sidenote number={6}>Fisher, *The Development of the Orshansky Poverty Thresholds and Their Subsequent History as the Official U.S. Poverty Measure* (1997).</Sidenote>

### The epistemological inversion

A population statistic typically begins by distributing people along a common scale and asking what pattern emerges. Orshansky's thresholds began with a household's food budget and asked whether its income could meet it. The food budget came first; the threshold followed. The household came first; the poverty rate followed. At every stage the measure was anchored in a published figure: the cost of the USDA's Economy Food Plan for a household of a given size and composition, the food-to-income ratio observed in the 1955 Household Food Consumption Survey, the difference between farm and non-farm food costs.<sup aria-describedby="sn-7">7</sup> Remove the specificity and the measure collapses. That was the point.

<Sidenote number={7}>Orshansky, "Counting the Poor: Another Look at the Poverty Profile," *Social Security Bulletin* (January 1965), pp. 3–8; Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

Ian Hacking's account of "making up people" explains why the form of the measure matters. Human classifications do not simply sort pre-existing kinds of people; the category and the people in it change one another — the "looping effect" that separates human kinds from natural ones. Quarks do not read what physicists say about them. People read what welfare administrators say about them, and adjust their lives accordingly.<sup aria-describedby="sn-8">8</sup> So the kind of people a poverty line makes up depends on the kind of measure it is. Orshansky's 124 thresholds made up households whose poverty could be described with precision — households that could, in principle, contest their classification by pointing at a grocery receipt and saying "this is wrong." The 1981 revision did not turn them into a single cutoff: it kept 48 cells distinguished by family size and composition, while removing farm residence and the sex of the household head.<sup aria-describedby="sn-9">9</sup> It still made up distinguishable households. It made up fewer of them, and carried forward fewer of the circumstances that had made the original categories material.

<Sidenote number={8}>Hacking, "Making Up People" (1986).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={9}>Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

Theodore Porter supplies the other half of the analysis. Quantification in public life, he argues, is a technology of trust: numbers gain authority from impersonal mechanical rules that make decisions appear objective.<sup aria-describedby="sn-10">10</sup> Orshansky's matrix is Porter's counter-case — quantification that resists depoliticisation. Its inputs and formula were published and reproducible; anyone who disagreed with the food budget or the multiplier could say precisely where and why; and it was derived from household consumption data rather than from a fiscal target or a political negotiation. Those properties made the matrix scientifically stronger and administratively inconvenient. The later series could be applied more uniformly across agencies. It gained impersonality by leaving fewer of a household's assumptions open to challenge.

<Sidenote number={10}>Porter, *Trust in Numbers* (1995).</Sidenote>

The kitchen knew something a statistical office could easily lose: poverty is particular. It varies by household, resists a single summary figure, and looks different from inside the kitchen than from inside the bureau. Orshansky's matrices made that difference visible. The question was whether an institution that needed standard measures could keep it visible.

## 2. From Beveridge to the Poverty Line: The Postwar Demand for a Number

The postwar welfare state could count contributors, claimants, and expenditure without measuring whether any particular household had enough to live on. William Beveridge's *Social Insurance and Allied Services* (1942) built a system around the question "who is insured?" rather than "who is poor?": flat-rate contributions from employers and employees, and flat-rate benefits paid as of right for sickness, unemployment, maternity, retirement, and funeral costs.<sup aria-describedby="sn-11">11</sup> The Government Actuary's Department checked contribution rates against projected benefit costs. The benefit rates themselves — 24 shillings a week for a single adult, 40 for a married couple — came from political judgment and actuarial constraint, not from a household-by-household inquiry into food, rent, fuel, and clothing.<sup aria-describedby="sn-12">12</sup> The scheme could tell the Treasury how many people were claiming and what it cost. It could not tell anyone whether a family of four in York or a retired woman in Stepney had enough to eat. In James Scott's terms, the flat rates made claimants legible in a form the post office could administer; the price of that legibility was the household variation the rates never asked about.<sup aria-describedby="sn-13">13</sup>

<Sidenote number={11}>Beveridge, *Social Insurance and Allied Services* (1942).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={12}>Harris, *William Beveridge: A Biography* (1997).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={13}>Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998).</Sidenote>

The report also concealed a collaboration. Ann Oakley's archival work recovers Janet Philip's editorial and research contribution, which the published document does not name. Beveridge himself later acknowledged that "it would be idle to separate what came from her mind and what from his."<sup aria-describedby="sn-14">14</sup> The mechanisms of disappearance differ: Philip's prose was absorbed into the report, leaving no seam at which a reader can say "this sentence is hers"; Orshansky's household distinctions would later be pared from the official series, leaving a number whose original assumptions drop out of view. In both cases the public instrument hides the work that made it possible.

<Sidenote number={14}>Oakley, *Forgotten Wives: How Women Get Written Out of History* (2023).</Sidenote>

### The American landscape: partial measures, no standard

The American problem was different: there was no official federal poverty measure at all. The Social Security Act created old-age and unemployment insurance without a common household standard of adequacy. Bureau of Labor Statistics family budgets and experimental lines inside the Social Security Administration existed, but no programme had adopted any of them as an eligibility criterion.<sup aria-describedby="sn-15">15</sup> Seebohm Rowntree's York surveys had shown what an empirical poverty line could look like — a nutritional minimum priced in local markets and scaled to household size, deliberately set at a floor "that could not be challenged for its generosity" — but they remained academic inquiries.<sup aria-describedby="sn-16">16</sup>

<Sidenote number={15}>Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={16}>Rowntree, *Poverty: A Study of Town Life* (1901).</Sidenote>

In 1963, Walter Heller's Council of Economic Advisers used a single \$3,000 annual family income for its national poverty count.<sup aria-describedby="sn-17">17</sup> The figure did not vary by household size or composition. Orshansky objected that one threshold for all family sizes systematically understated the number of children in poverty relative to elderly people, because a larger family needs more income to meet the same basic needs. A national yardstick could count people. It could not distinguish the circumstances in which they lived.

<Sidenote number={17}>Heller, Council of Economic Advisers (1963); Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

### The SSA corridor and a household standard

In July 1963, Orshansky published "Children of the Poor" in the *Social Security Bulletin*, using household food costs to construct an income standard for families with children. Two years later she described the exercise plainly: not a final statement of adequate income, but a way to say "how much, on an average, is too little."<sup aria-describedby="sn-18">18</sup> The Economy Food Plan supplied the food budget; the 1955 survey supplied the ratio from which an income threshold could be derived. In Britain, Peter Townsend would later approach deprivation from the other direction, asking what social participation a person could sustain rather than what a household could eat — a contrast that clarifies both the reach and the limits of a food-budget threshold.<sup aria-describedby="sn-19">19</sup> For the first time, a federal statistician had supplied a household-specific measure of poverty. Its arithmetic began with the cost of feeding a particular family.

<Sidenote number={18}>Orshansky 1963; Orshansky 1965, p. 3.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={19}>Townsend, *Poverty in the United Kingdom* (1979).</Sidenote>

## 3. The Matrices: Transparency as a Mathematical Property

What Orshansky published in January 1965 was an economy-level schedule of 124 thresholds, extending the method of "Children of the Poor" to a wider set of family types and unrelated individuals. She stated the principle directly: the cash income required changes between "the family of two and the family of eight," and between metropolitan and farm residence.<sup aria-describedby="sn-20">20</sup> A poverty rate divides a population in two: poor and not poor. A threshold schedule differentiates within the population it measures, preserving the fact that poverty in a two-person elderly household on an Iowa farm is not the same as poverty in a household of eight in a Philadelphia row house. The rate asks how many. The schedule asks who, specifically, and under what material conditions.

<Sidenote number={20}>Orshansky 1965, p. 3; Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

### The method: from food to income

The calculation has three steps, and each rests on a published observation.

**A food budget.** The USDA published four tiered food plans — Economy, Low-Cost, Moderate-Cost, and Liberal — each derived from observed household purchasing patterns and adjusted to meet the National Research Council's recommended daily allowances. They were costed baskets: specific quantities of specific foods, priced in retail markets, calculated for households of different size and composition.<sup aria-describedby="sn-21">21</sup> Orshansky chose the Economy plan, the cheapest. The choice was deliberate and political, like Rowntree's choice of a nutritional minimum in York: she wanted a floor so austere that no critic could dismiss the people below it as improvident. She described the plan herself as intended for "temporary or emergency use when funds are low."<sup aria-describedby="sn-22">22</sup> It assumed a household with the knowledge, equipment, time, and skill to cook every meal from scratch and waste nothing. Anyone below this floor was poor by the most conservative nutritional criterion the federal government published.

<Sidenote number={21}>USDA, Economy Food Plan (1961 methodology).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={22}>Orshansky 1963; Orshansky 1965, p. 5.</Sidenote>

**A ratio.** The 1955 Household Food Consumption Survey found that families of three or more spent about one-third of their after-tax income on food.<sup aria-describedby="sn-23">23</sup> This was not a claim about what families should spend. It was an observation about what they did spend.

<Sidenote number={23}>USDA, *Household Food Consumption Survey, 1955*, Report No. 1.</Sidenote>

**An inversion.** If food is a third of income, the minimum income a household needs is three times the cost of its minimum food budget. Orshansky multiplied the Economy Food Plan cost for each household type by three. For families of three or more, poverty meant income below that product; for a family of two adults and two young children, it came to \$3,100 a year off the farm and \$1,860 on it.<sup aria-describedby="sn-24">24</sup> One- and two-person units used special procedures and ratios. The multiplier was not a guess: a household that could not afford three times the minimum food basket could not, by the survey's own evidence, eat at that minimum and still pay for rent, fuel, clothing, transport, and medical care.

<Sidenote number={24}>Orshansky 1965, pp. 3, 8; Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

The method's economy was epistemic. A critic who thought the Economy Food Plan too generous or too austere could say which foods, at which prices, in which quantities, they would change. A critic who thought the one-third ratio had shifted, or varied too much by household size, could point to the survey data. A critic who doubted that food costs were a reliable proxy for total need could make that argument and propose an alternative. The inputs were published, the arithmetic was reproducible, and the assumptions were named. This is the inverse of what Porter calls a "technology of distance," where rule-following substitutes for judgment and so makes a decision unchallengeable. The CEA's \$3,000 figure, by contrast, combined the minimum wage, tax liability, and Aid to Families with Dependent Children payments, and did not vary by household size at all.<sup aria-describedby="sn-25">25</sup>

<Sidenote number={25}>Porter 1995; Heller 1963; Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

### What the dimensions recorded

The schedule distinguished households by five characteristics, each recording a material fact about how households lived.

- **Size.** Food, fuel, and space cost more for seven people than for one, but not seven times more. The thresholds tracked those economies of scale: the threshold for seven people was lower per person and higher in total.
- **Age of household head.** The Economy Food Plan cost less for elderly households, so their thresholds were lower — not because elderly poverty was less severe but because the food floor was. The distinction survived 1969, though its meaning changed once CPI indexing severed it from the food-plan data that justified it.
- **Children.** "Children of the Poor" carried that title because the \$3,000 line treated two adults and two adults with four children as the same household. The matrix gave each configuration of adults and children its own threshold. It made children visible in the poverty count; the single line hid them.
- **Sex of household head.** The schedule set separate thresholds for male- and female-headed households of otherwise similar composition. The distinction survived 1969 and was removed in 1981, when the separate sets were merged.
- **Farm residence.** Farm families grew some of their own food. Orshansky set farm thresholds at 60 per cent of the non-farm figures; the Office of Economic Opportunity raised that to 70 per cent in 1965; the 1969 revision raised it to 85 per cent; and in 1981 the non-farm thresholds were applied to everyone.<sup aria-describedby="sn-26">26</sup> Farm and non-farm households still differed. The series stopped recording it.

<Sidenote number={26}>USDA 1961; Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

Together these produced 124 categories, each visible to the state in a different way. An elderly woman living alone on a farm was a different kind of poor from a young mother with three children in a city apartment, and the matrix said so in a number the state could use.

### Four properties

**Transparency.** The Economy Food Plan was a public document with itemised quantities and prices; the 1955 survey was public; the multiplication was one step. There was no proprietary model and no unexplained coefficient. The threshold for a non-farm family of four with two children could be recalculated from scratch by anyone with access to a library and a pencil.

**Contestability.** Because the inputs were published and the arithmetic simple, anyone who disagreed could locate the precise point of disagreement — a nutritionist could name the foods she would add, an economist could cite later survey data showing the food share had fallen, a demographer could bring rural budgets to bear on the farm adjustment. A contestable measure, in Porter's terms, distributes the power to challenge it rather than concentrating the power to assert it.

**Material grounding.** The threshold was derived from something a household had to do — eat — at a level the federal government's own nutritionists defined as the minimum. It was not derived from a fiscal target, a presidential announcement, or a position in the income distribution. It was an empirical claim, and it could be wrong; but it could be wrong in a way that was checkable rather than arbitrary.

**Granularity.** Each distinction corresponded to a real variation in what households needed. A measure that treats poverty as one thing — a single line below which everyone is equally poor — has already decided not to see the differences that matter most to the people living them.

These were the properties Orshansky built, and they were the properties that made her schedule hard to administer. A transparent measure can be challenged; a contestable one invites argument; a materially grounded one must be updated when material conditions change; a granular one is harder to run than a simple one. After 1965, the need for common definitions, manageable reporting, and a stable series became the reason for narrowing them.

## 4. Political Battles and the Cost of Complexity

Heller needed a number that could mobilise political will. Orshansky needed a measure that could tell which children were poor. Her objection to the CEA's flat \$3,000 was methodological, not merely political: a household of two adults and four children needed far more income than a household of two elderly people, and a line that treated them as equivalent could tell a president how large his problem was without identifying who had it. The line counted heads. The matrix counted mouths.

The Office of Economic Opportunity — Sargent Shriver's War on Poverty agency — adopted Orshansky's economy-level thresholds in May 1965 as its working definition of poverty, and the Census Bureau began using them for its annual statistics soon after.<sup aria-describedby="sn-27">27</sup> But adoption was quasi-official. Different agencies adjusted the thresholds differently, producing inconsistent poverty counts that embarrassed the administration and confused the public.

<Sidenote number={27}>Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

### The Bureau of the Budget intervenes

On 29 August 1969 the Bureau of the Budget directed federal Executive-Branch agencies to use the revised Census thresholds for statistical purposes, making them the government's official statistical definition of poverty.<sup aria-describedby="sn-28">28</sup> The directive did not replace the matrix with one national figure. It formalised two revisions.

<Sidenote number={28}>Fisher 1997.</Sidenote>

The first was CPI indexing. Until 1969 the thresholds were updated each year by the change in the per-capita cost of the Economy Food Plan. In 1968 the SSA had proposed revised food plans based on 1965 survey data, and a higher multiplier from the same survey was under discussion; together the changes would have raised the thresholds by 25 to 30 per cent. The interagency committee instead applied the Consumer Price Index to the 1963 non-farm thresholds. The thresholds still bore their food-budget derivation, but their movement over time was now detached from the food plan and the consumption evidence that had justified them. A price index can reproduce a series indefinitely without ever testing whether the underlying food budget, or the food-to-income ratio, still describes what households need. Revisiting either became a separate policy decision that the routine no longer required anyone to make.

The second revision narrowed the farm differential from 70 to 85 per cent of the non-farm figures. The sex-of-household-head distinction survived. Both went in 1981, when the detailed matrix was cut to 48 cells and the separate thresholds for male- and female-headed households were averaged into one.

### Orshansky objects

Orshansky did not object to a consistent series. She objected to consistency standing in for a renewed judgment about need. In a 1970 newspaper interview she said the 1969 decision "tends to freeze the poverty line despite changes in buying habits and changes in acceptable living standards."<sup aria-describedby="sn-29">29</sup> The phrase identifies the problem exactly: a threshold can be stable enough to compare across years while becoming steadily less adequate as an account of what households require.

<Sidenote number={29}>Quoted in Fisher 1997, p. 28.</Sidenote>

An updated measure was technically available. In 1979, Orshansky and Carol Fendler set out an updated measure based on the USDA's Thrifty Food Plan and a multiplier of 3.4 derived from the 1965 Household Food Consumption Survey.<sup aria-describedby="sn-30">30</sup> They treated the food plan, the multiplier, and the household categories as assumptions that should stay open to evidence and revision. Once a fixed CPI rule governed the threshold's annual movement, the question of whether it still fitted household life had no place in its routine. By 1981 the series applied one threshold to farm and non-farm households, and to households headed by women and by men. It was more uniform. It no longer required anyone to revisit the food budget or the household differences that had produced it.

<Sidenote number={30}>Fendler and Orshansky, "Improving the Poverty Definition" (1979); Fisher 1997, p. 42.</Sidenote>

## 5. Thresholds as Governance, Then and Now

The US poverty measure now has two related forms that do different institutional work. The Census Bureau's official poverty thresholds are a statistical yardstick: they vary by family size and composition, do not vary by place, and are updated each year using CPI-U.<sup aria-describedby="sn-31">31</sup> The Department of Health and Human Services' poverty guidelines are a simplified administrative version of those thresholds, also updated by CPI-U, and used by Medicaid and other federal programmes to determine eligibility.<sup aria-describedby="sn-32">32</sup> Both descend from the method Orshansky developed in 1963 and extended in 1965. Their annual movement has followed a price index since 1969, and their structure was cut down again in 1981.

<Sidenote number={31}>US Census Bureau, "How the Census Bureau Measures Poverty."</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={32}>HHS, "Annual Update of the HHS Poverty Guidelines" (2026).</Sidenote>

The threshold is a fossil in a precise sense: its movement is governed by a price index rather than by any built-in reassessment of the food plan, the multiplier, and the household distinctions from which it was derived. That preserves a comparable series. It does not answer whether the series still represents the conditions it is meant to measure. This is a problem of governance rather than of knowledge. Statistical thresholds and administrative guidelines serve different ends, but both reward a stable, repeatable rule, and Porter's technology of distance explains their authority: an impersonal number makes a decision look objective even after the material assumptions behind it have dropped from view. Orshansky's thresholds invited argument because their inputs were published and their assumptions named. CPI indexing makes the annual adjustment mechanical. It does not settle the prior question of whether the inherited threshold still answers to household need.

### The Supplemental Poverty Measure

In 2011 the Census Bureau, working with the Bureau of Labor Statistics and drawing on the recommendations of a 1995 National Academy of Sciences panel, began publishing the Supplemental Poverty Measure alongside the official one. The SPM builds its threshold from a rolling five-year average of spending on food, clothing, shelter, and utilities; adjusts it for local housing costs; counts noncash benefits as resources; and subtracts taxes, work expenses, and out-of-pocket medical costs.<sup aria-describedby="sn-33">33</sup> These are not neutral corrections. They change the poverty count by redefining what counts as a resource, what counts as a necessary expense, and where a household's need is located. And the institutional line holds: HHS's 2026 guidelines are still derived from the official thresholds, not from the SPM.<sup aria-describedby="sn-34">34</sup> The better-grounded measure sits beside the administrative series without choosing the number that decides who qualifies for Medicaid.

<Sidenote number={33}>US Census Bureau, *The Supplemental Poverty Measure: 2011*.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={34}>HHS 2026.</Sidenote>

### Universal Credit's standard allowances

In Britain, the Department for Work and Pensions publishes Universal Credit's standard allowances as a rate table. For 2026–27 the monthly amounts are £338.58 for a single claimant under 25, £424.90 for a single claimant aged 25 or over, £528.34 for joint claimants both under 25, and £666.97 where one or both are 25 or over, with child and disability amounts listed separately.<sup aria-describedby="sn-35">35</sup> These figures rose by more than inflation in April 2026: the Universal Credit Act 2025 requires the standard allowance to be increased each year to 2029–30 by CPI plus a fixed statutory uplift, 2.3 per cent for 2026–27.<sup aria-describedby="sn-36">36</sup>

<Sidenote number={35}>DWP, *Benefit and Pension Rates 2026 to 2027*. The 2025–26 amounts were £316.98, £400.14, £497.55, and £628.10 (DWP, *Benefit and Pension Rates 2025 to 2026*).</Sidenote>

<Sidenote number={36}>Universal Credit Act 2025 (c. 22). The uplift rises to 3.1, 4.0, and 4.8 per cent in the three following years.</Sidenote>

The rate table makes the size of an award public. It does not, by itself, show how the levels were set or whether they match what a household's life costs — and the 2025 Act, for all that it raises the allowance, does so by a price index plus a percentage written into statute, not by a published account of what a single adult or a couple needs to eat, heat a home, and stay clothed. The comparison with Orshansky is not a claim of transatlantic descent; Universal Credit has its own institutional history. It is a comparison of the evidence each record puts in front of a reader. Her 1965 article set out a food plan, a multiplier, and thresholds differentiated by household type. The DWP table states amounts. Only one of them gives a critic the materials to argue about adequacy on the measure's own terms.

### What Orshansky left

In a 1988 commentary Orshansky did not treat the poverty line as settled. "Many difficulties with the original measure remain unresolved," she wrote. The Current Population Survey still recorded income before taxes; the price adjuster "does not necessarily apply to the poor"; and nothing kept the index in step with changing consumption. "Yesterday's luxuries quickly become tomorrow's necessities."<sup aria-describedby="sn-37">37</sup>

<Sidenote number={37}>Orshansky, commentary in *Social Security Bulletin* (1988), p. 23.</Sidenote>

The record she left contains revisions as well as objections: the 1968 food-plan-and-multiplier proposal that would have raised the thresholds by a quarter or more; the 1979 Thrifty Food Plan measure with its 3.4 multiplier. What survives is not a timeless line but a method whose assumptions remain available for revision — a standard for the work itself. A poverty measure can be transparent about its inputs, differentiated by household circumstances, and open to revision when the evidence changes. A stable series has its uses. It cannot relieve the institutions that run it of the duty to decide whether its inherited assumptions still describe what households need.

### Connection Forward

Chapter 2 turns to the statistical tradition Orshansky was working against: the tools Galton and Pearson developed not to describe human variation but to rank it, and whose grammar — the normal curve, the correlation coefficient, the z-score — became so embedded in quantitative social science that its eugenic origins disappeared from view. The kitchen table where Orshansky began is not simply a counter-image to Galton's laboratory in South Kensington. It is a methodological counter-claim: to measure what households need rather than where they rank in a distribution.
